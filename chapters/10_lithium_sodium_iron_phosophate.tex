\chapter{Studying intercalation dynamics via non-stationary impedance}
\label{chap:LTP}

The previous chapter demonstrated the applicability of dynamic impedance spectroscopy for characterizing the aging of batteries as black-box systems, but the method is not limited to full cells. The rest of this thesis will show how the methodology unlocks new ways of characterizing active materials and single electrodes as white-box systems.
The project discussed in this section deals with the characterization of intercalation processes in lithium sodium titanium phosphate as a negative electrode for aqueous sodium-ion batteries. The non-stationary impedance of the electrode was estimated during constant current and voltage-sweep drifts. The differences between these two approaches are analyzed along with physical interpretation. The results of this project led to publication in a peer-reviewed article \cite{scarpioniElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2023a}.

\section{Materials and cell assembly}

\subsection{Active material}

% LIthium titaniumphosphate
% carbon containing
% particle saize, surface area measurements
% Synthesis route (brief)
% Why using it for Na-ion intercalation (NASICON structure, fast Na diffusion and widepotential window in water-in-salt)

Carbon-coated LiTi$_2$(PO$_4$)$_3$ (LTP) was synthesized via a one-pot route as reported in \cite{scarpioniElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2023a}. Briefly, a gel was prepared by dissolving ammonium dihydrogen phosphate and titanium butoxide in ethanol together with lithium acetate and glucose as lithium and carbon sources. After evaporation of the solvent, the powder was calcined at 800$^\circ$C in inert atmosphere. The carbon coating is needed to enhance the electrode's electronic conductivity. LTP has a NASICON-type framework, providing fast ion diffusion and excellent stability in aqueous electrolytes. Its potential window is suitable for negative electrode operation in water-in-salt systems because it remains safely above the hydrogen evolution reaction.

\subsection{Electrolyte}
% Describe the composition of water-in-salt and why using it

We used a so-called water-in-salt electrolyte based on sodium acetate (7~mol\,kg$^{-1}$) mixed with potassium acetate (20~mol\,kg$^{-1}$) in water. This high concentration extends water's electrochemical stability window by reducing solvent activity. Such superconcentrated electrolytes show relatively high ionic conductivity (around 20\,mS\,cm$^{-1}$ at room temperature) and suppress side reactions typical of more dilute aqueous solutions.

\subsection{Electrode preparation}
% Loading, binder, carbon, substrate
% Geometry

A slurry of carbon-coated LTP (80\,wt\%), carbon black (10\,wt\%) and PVDF binder (10\,wt\%) was prepared in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone. This slurry was dip-coated onto a carbon cloth substrate to form the working electrode with a loading of about 6\,mg\,cm$^{-2}$. After drying, the electrode was cut into 1x4cm strips with 1x1 cm of exposed active material.

\subsection{Three-electrode flooded cell}
% Reference electrode (Ag/AgCl) and placement
% Counter electrode
% Schematic of the cell (figure)

All measurements were performed in a three-electrode glass cell. A Pt mesh was used as the counter electrode, while a Ag/AgCl electrode served as reference and was positioned behind the counter electrode.
A schematic representation of the three-electrode flooded cell, including reference electrode placement relative to the counter electrode, is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:3elecsetup}.

\section{DEIS measurement setup}
% How the potentiostat is wired to the 3-electrode cell

The setup for dynamic impedance measurements consisted of a BioLogic SP-300 potentiostat, a Keysight TrueForm 33522B arbitrary waveform generator, and a PicoTech Picoscope 4000a for voltage and current signal recording.
Since this work preceded the acquisition automation, the waveform was loaded via USB into the waveform generator as a CSV file (in \emph{.arb} format). The signal length was limited by the 16 GB of RAM of the laboratory computer, so only two cycles with DEIS could be recorded.

\subsection{Multi-sine excitation}
% Frequenies: 0.1 Hz - 10 kHz
% Number of points per decade
% Phase randomization
% Amplitude selection procedure
% Duration of one multisine period

We generated a multi-sine waveform with frequencies from 0.1\,Hz to 10\,kHz with 8 points per decade, using only non-intermodulating integer multiples of the fundamental frequency, which also defines the period of 10 s. The sine component amplitudes were initially estimated from a preliminary EIS measurement to ensure a high signal-to-noise ratio without interfering with the main drift. The phases of individual sine components were randomized to minimize the crest factor. 

\subsection{Applied drift}
% Galvanostatic (CC): 138 mA/g (1C), continuous drift
% Cyclic voltammetry (CV): 0.8 mV/s
% Justify why these two drifts probe different physics:
% CC → concentration gradient development
% CV → potential-controlled interface dynamics
% Different “instantaneous” operating points in the i-V plane

We superimposed the multi-sine onto either a galvanostatic or a cyclic-voltammetry drift. The galvanostatic measurement was performed at 138\,mA\,g$^{-1}$ (approximately 1C) so that the voltage swept across the intercalation region in approximately two hours. In the CV experiment, we applied a 0.8\,mV\,s$^{-1}$ scan rate over a similar time span, ensuring a fair comparison with the constant current test. Both strategies place the electrode at different instantaneous operational points in the $i$--$V$ space: galvanostatic mode highlights concentration gradient formation and depletion, while CV is governed by potential-driven interface processes. Multi-frequency voltage and current signals were sampled at 50\,kHz. 
%Because each experiment lasted about two hours, the total amount of recorded points was on the order of $3.6\times 10^8$, including an integer multiple of multisine periods.

\subsection{Impedance estimation}
% DMFA
% Bandwidth 0.1 Hz
% Choice of n = 8
% Block size
% Removal of DC trends

The impedance was estimated offline using Dynamic Multi-Frequency Analysis with the symmetric Fermi-Dirac filter of bandwidth 0.1\,Hz and order $n=8$. Hence, the time resolution of the impedance is 10 s. The DMFA was applied to the entire signal of two consecutive cycles after subtracting the straight line connecting the first and last points.

% Comment on asymmetry
A notable observation is the contrast in impedance measured during ion insertion versus removal. The larger adsorption resistance and depressed semicircle during oxidation suggest that the energy barrier for desolvation and adsorption differs from that of desorption and re-solvation. Although both processes occur over a similar potential region, the system appears kinetically more hindered during oxidation. This asymmetry underscores the advantage of dynamic impedance in detecting differences that equilibrium EIS might obscure.
Interestingly, an asymmetry in shape emerges when switching from intercalation (negative current) to deintercalation (positive current). The semicircle during oxidation appears more depressed at the beginning and then recovers the same shape as reduction. The time-dependent resistance associated with ion adsorption onto the electrode surface changes more significantly during oxidation. This suggests non-symmetric free energies of ion insertion versus extraction, consistent with earlier reports of asymmetry in alkali-ion adsorption processes.

\subsection{Model fitting}
In order to extract quantitative information about the electrode processes, we fitted the dynamic impedance spectra with a porous-electrode-based model, adapted from Reference \cite{scarpioniElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2023a}. The approach is grounded in the simplified transmission-line concept for intercalation electrodes, which includes terms for ohmic resistance, interfacial kinetics, and finite-length diffusion or pseudocapacitive behavior.

The porous-electrode model used to interpret the dynamic impedance data, including its lumped parameters and their physical meaning, is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:LTP_model}.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/LTP/model parameters together with letters.png}
    \caption{Porous-electrode model fitting.
    Parameters evolve with the state of charge, changing mainly when the electrode potential crosses the plateau.
    Abrupt jumps in some parameters are observed when the current is reversed, since the double layer has no time to relax to OCV conditions.}
    \label{fig:LTP_model}
\end{figure}

We carried out the fitting using the library \verb|pymultipleis|, which fits all spectra simultaneously by treating the problem as multidimensional (number of spectra times the number of parameters).

\subsection{Time-resolved impedance under CV drift}

In the cyclic voltammetry experiment, the potential scans at 0.8\,mV\,s$^{-1}$, and the electrode can momentarily reach kinetic regimes strongly influenced by concentration polarization. This manifests in the DEIS data as a pronounced rise in impedance, especially around the lower plateau, where transport limitations are accentuated. The real-time spectra show more pronounced distortions in the low-frequency region, associated with the voltage-driven, layer-by-layer intercalation behavior.

\subsection{Direct comparison}
% why impedance look different depending on the system?

Comparing the CC and CV results highlights how different drifts can emphasize different kinetic contributions. Under galvanostatic conditions, the electrode must sustain the set current, so the impedance shape largely reflects the electrode's instantaneous ability to supply or accept ions. Under sweep-voltage (CV) conditions, the potential is the control variable, and mass-transport or pseudocapacitive features can dominate as the electrode passes through distinct depletion and accumulation states.

\section{Impedance validation}
To ensure that the estimated instantaneous impedance spectra satisfy the LTI assumption, we fitted the spectra with a general transfer function in the form of a rational polynomial of order $n$. To find the proper model structure differnt orders ranging from 3 to 8 were tested on the galvanostatic (CC) dataset, and from 3 to 9 for the sweep-voltage (CV) dataset, aiming to find the best compromise between goodness of fit and model simplicity.
The fitting procedure was carried out with \verb|pymultieis| \footnote{Available at https://github.com/richinex/pymultieis}. 
The Akaike information criterion (AIC) was calculated for each polynomial order, and its minimum value identified the optimal complexity level. As reported in Figure \ref{fig:LTP_AIC}, orders 3 and 4 underfit several sections of the spectra, whereas orders 7 and 8 add marginal improvements but introduce unnecessary parameters. The AIC ultimately indicated order 6 to be optimal for the CC data and order 8 for the CV data.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/LTP/AIC_CC_and_CV.png}
    \caption{Model-order selection via Akaike Information Criterion.
    AIC values computed for Padé polynomial fits of increasing order for galvanostatic (CC) and cyclic voltammetry (CV) datasets. The minima identify the optimal trade-off between goodness of fit and model complexity.}
    \label{fig:LTP_AIC}
\end{figure}

With the proper model structure identified, we assessed that the error was less than 0.4\% of the impedance magnitude which was for us a satisfactory LTI test.
The result of the fitting and residuals are reported in the Appendix \ref{app:validation LTP}.

\section{Summary and perspective}
% These experiments demonstrate the mechanistic power of DEIS
% The aim was not full materials characterization but method validation
% Intercalation electrodes act as a clean system to validate the methodology’s ability to resolve:
% -diffusion
% -charge-transfer
% -drift-induced nonstationarity
% -directional asymmetry
% This justifies the next step: applying DEIS to full batteries to study degradation and SOH

These experiments demonstrate that non-stationary impedance spectroscopy can separate different reaction steps for an intercalation material under realistic battery operating conditions.
By comparing galvanostatic and voltage-sweep measurements, we showed that the electrode's time-resolved impedance reveals differences in diffusion, interfacial kinetics, and their directional asymmetry. 

While the primary goal here was to validate DEIS rather than to fully characterize LTP, the physical model fitting revealed quantitative values for charge-transfer, adsorption, and transport processes. These results provided insight to formulate a hypothesis on asymmetry in solvation and desolvation processes that would not have been accessible via direct current methods alone. 

The limitation of this study was the short measurement time, limited by the computer RAM, and also the physical model not being entirely sufficient to explain the system dynamics. Furthermore, characterization at the material level must be carried out with information from multiple techniques, not only electrochemical, to draw the full picture of the material properties.

The following development involves measuring non-stationary impedance of batteries and using it for aging identification. This is the reason no in-depth study of the electrochemistry of porous activated carbon and carbon-coated lithium titanium phosphate was continued. Nevertheless, I hope that this preliminary work might inspire scientists interested in mechanistic understanding of such systems to replicate our methodology.
