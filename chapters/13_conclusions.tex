\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{ch:conclusions}

This thesis addressed a fundamental limitation of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy when applied to realistic energy storage systems: the incompatibility between the classical assumption of stationarity and the intrinsically dynamic nature of batteries during operation. 
While impedance spectroscopy is widely recognized as a powerful diagnostic tool, its practical use has remained largely confined to equilibrium or quasi-equilibrium conditions, limiting its ability to probe processes that evolve continuously during cycling. 
The central objective of this work was therefore to establish a complete methodology for broadband impedance characterization under non-stationary conditions.

By combining frequency-domain system identification theory with electrochemical insight and digital signal processing, this thesis demonstrated that meaningful impedance information can be extracted from evolving systems. 
The work clarified the conceptual distinction between classical impedance and instantaneous impedance of a time-varying system, and showed how multi-frequency excitation and time-resolved estimation techniques allow this estimation in practice.

A complete experimental framework for dynamic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy was developed and validated. 
This framework integrates optimized multisine excitation, synchronized high-speed acquisition, online signal processing, and automated experiment control. 
Particular attention was devoted to overcoming practical limitations that have historically hindered the adoption of multisine-based impedance methods, including waveform memory constraints, data volume management, and long experiment duration. 
The resulting platform enables continuous impedance acquisition over seven decades of frequency during multi-week battery cycling experiments, while reducing raw data storage requirements by several orders of magnitude through online processing.

The methodology was validated across a range of electrochemical systems and configurations. 
Measurements on commercial coin cells demonstrated that continuous impedance acquisition during cycling yields results consistent with stationary measurements, while providing access to the evolution of impedance features across state of charge and state of health. 
Single-electrode studies further showed that the approach is applicable to both Faradaic and non-Faradaic systems, and that low-frequency processes relevant to diffusion and interfacial phenomena can be accessed under non-stationary conditions. 
These applications served not as isolated case studies, but as validation of the generality and robustness of the proposed framework.

Beyond specific experimental results, this work delivers a set of open-source software tools that make advanced dynamic impedance measurements accessible using standard laboratory equipment. 
By lowering the technical barrier to long-duration, broadband, non-stationary impedance experiments, the framework enables future studies to focus on physical interpretation, model development, and data-driven analysis.

Overall, this thesis establishes dynamic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy as a practical and scalable tool for operando characterization of batteries and related electrochemical systems. 
While the present work focused on developing and validating the measurement methodology itself, it lays the foundation for future research aimed at linking time-resolved impedance features to degradation mechanisms, control strategies, and predictive models for battery health and performance.

The measurement shows reproducibility over 9 cells and the analysis shows no alteration of the aging caused by the multi-sine excitation when compared to the control experiment set.

% % Other measurements
% Dynamic impedance spectroscopy is not restricted to full cell applications. In this work I also included experiments for the electrochemical characterization of single electrodes for Faradaic and non-Faradaic energy storage types in three-electrode laboratory cells. In this case, the separation of the electrodes impedance by the presence of a reference electrode makes the physical interpretation easier and enabled extraction of kinetics parameters using existing physical models.

% The first case-study was lithium titanium phosphate for sodium ion intercalation as possible active material in aqueous sodium-ion battery negative electrodes. Here we showed the difference in the impedance obtained when the drift is a constant current, typical for battery characterization, or cyclic voltage sweep, typical in electroanalytics. The instantaneous impedance of the working electrode shows how the surface kinetics is slowed down when the peak of the voltammetry is approached and suggests why intercalation materials should be studied with the constant current technique. 

% We fitted a physical model for ion intercalation with porous electrode theory to the data of one charge and discharge cycle with galvanostatic drift to estimate the kinetics parameters. The most interesting finding was the asymmetry in the charge transfer process between charge and discharge that we interpreted as a difference in solvation and desolvation activation energy. 

% The other case-study was an electrical double layer capacitor made of symmetric activated porous carbon electrodes in an aqueous solution of KF in a three-electrode Swagelok type cell. Here, we experimented with the instrumental set-up using two channels of a potentiostat with one scope each for the acquisition of full cell and half cell voltages that allows for the separated estimation of the impedance for positive and negative electrode. Such experiment demonstrates that even in symmetric systems the impedance of the electrodes is different because of the history and electric potential. This type of experiment can also identify cross-talk between the electrodes while the electrochemical device is cycling. In fact, the second channel of the workstation is used only to measure the voltage drop of one half-cell (the other is deduced by subtraction with the full-cell voltage).

% Another interesting result is about the position of the reference electrode. On the same cell, we positioned the tip of the reference electrode outside the electrodes stack and then in the middle of it, between two separators, obtaining the appearance of an artifact in the impedance in the first case as obtained in some theoretical investigation.

% We used again a physical model for the adsorption of ions on porous electrodes to estimate the kinetics parameters from the impedance data, although it doesn't change significantly during the cycling.

% In these two experiments we focused only on the impedance change within a cycle since at that time the instrumental set-up was not yet automatized. I suggest a follow-up project in which the impedance of single interfaces in three-electrode cells is measured over multiple cycles to characterize the degradation.

With the pouch cell geometry I conducted experiments on graphite negative electrode for lithium-ion intercalation and SiCN(O) negative electrode for sodium-ion insertion.

Another interesting observation from these experiments is the comparison of the impedance of the same electrode at different C-rates. The ohmic drop increases with the C-rate and the asymmetry between oxidation and reduction widens.
