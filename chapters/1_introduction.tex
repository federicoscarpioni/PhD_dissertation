\chapter{Introduction}

% Batteries and why impedance matters
The transition to electric mobility and renewable energy storage demands batteries with high energy density, fast charging capability, long operational lifetime and stringent safety requirements. Lithium-ion batteries have dominated the market for portable electronics and are now the technology of choice for electric vehicles, electrical grid stabilization and renewable energy storage. Yet, significant challenges remain in understanding and predicting their degradation mechanisms during operation. Battery aging is a complex interplay of processes occurring at multiple time scales from fast charge-transfer reactions at electrode-electrolyte interfaces (microseconds to milliseconds) to slow solid-state diffusion and mechanical degradation (seconds to hours). The understanding and prediction of a battery, a dynamical system, can be performed from characterization in the time or frequency domain; the latter, allowing a much more effective way of separating processes at multiple time scales with one simple experiment, is the subject of this work.

In electrochemistry, the frequency domain identification technique is referred to as electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), extensively used in battery research. By measuring the frequency-dependent response of a battery to small alternating current or voltage perturbations, EIS separates process responses based on their characteristic time constants \cite{orazemEIS}. A single impedance spectrum contains information about charge-transfer resistance, double-layer capacitance, ion diffusion, and adsorption phenomena across a frequency range typically spanning from mHz to kHz. This multi-scale view makes EIS invaluable for mechanistic studies and performance diagnosis.

% Current state and limitations
However, conventional EIS measurements require the battery to be in thermodynamic equilibrium, where the system state (the set of its properties) does not change during the measurement time. For a full spectrum covering six or seven decades of frequency, this requirement translates to measurement times of 30 minutes to several hours. During battery cycling, interrupting the charge or discharge to perform EIS introduces significant overhead time and alters the electrochemical and thermal state of the cell. As a result, operando impedance measurements have been limited to either: narrow frequency bands in the middle to high range (above 1 Hz), which sacrifices the information of transport, or sparse equilibrium measurements at fixed states of charge, which miss the continuous evolution across voltage and cycles \footnote{Cite our latest work in Current Opinion in Electrochemsitry}. This gap becomes critical when studying phenomena that evolve continuously during cycling: solid-electrolyte interphase growth, lithium plating, particle cracking, or state-dependent kinetics. Furthermore, measuring impedance during operation provides \emph{in situ} information under realistic electrochemical conditions (varying state of charge, current load, thermal state) that cannot be replicated in equilibrium measurements.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, however, is not merely an experimental technique but a system identification method with strict underlying assumptions. In its classical formulation, impedance is defined only for linear and time-invariant systems, implying that the electrochemical state must remain effectively constant over the duration of the measurement. Batteries operated under realistic conditions violate this assumption by construction: their state of charge, interfacial composition, temperature, and reaction rates evolve continuously during charge and discharge. As a consequence, extending impedance spectroscopy to operando battery measurements is not simply a matter of faster data acquisition, but requires rethinking how impedance is defined, estimated, and validated for non-stationary systems. This challenge motivates a unified treatment of electrochemistry, frequency-domain system theory, digital signal processing, and experimental instrumentation, which together form the conceptual foundation of this thesis.

\section{Scope}
% Proposed solution
This thesis addresses this gap by developing a complete methodology for broadband electrochemical impedance spectroscopy in non-stationary conditions. By combining commercial instrumentation with custom automation software and online signal processing, the setup enabled continuous impedance acquisition across seven decades of frequency (10 mHz to 100 kHz) throughout multi-week battery cycling experiments. The methodology removes the stationary-state requirement through multi-frequency perturbation techniques and time-resolved impedance estimation, while solving the practical challenges of data volume (reducing storage requirements by four orders of magnitude through online processing) and multi-channel operation. The open-source software stack makes this capability accessible to other research groups using standard laboratory equipment.

% Explain multidisciplinary nature
The development of this methodology required integrating knowledge from multiple disciplines. Electrochemistry, describing the physical processes at electrode-electrolyte interfaces, was necessary to design appropriate perturbation signals, interpret the resulting impedance spectra, and design reference electrodes. Battery technology knowledge informed the choice of active materials, cell geometries, and cycling protocols relevant to practical applications, as well as device performance evaluation. The hardware implementation demanded familiarity with potentiostat operation and electronics to configure it with a waveform generator and signal acquisition device. Digital signal processing theory was necessary for the design of multi-sine excitations, the implementation of Fourier transform-based impedance estimation, and strategies for managing spectral leakage and noise. System identification theory provided concepts for understanding the classification of systems by stationarity and linearity, and the concept of transfer function representations. Finally, the automation and online processing required advanced programming in Python, including multithreaded operation, efficient numerical computation with NumPy, and custom hardware interfaces, while the fitting of instantanous impedance spectra demanded expertise in mathematical optimization. This thesis therefore sits at the intersection of experimental electrochemistry, electrical engineering, and computational methods, reflecting the inherently multidisciplinary nature of modern battery research.

% Scope statement
The primary focus of this work was the development and validation of the measurement methodology itself. The thesis demonstrates the technique's applicability across multiple electrochemical energy storage systems: commercial batteries, laboratory cells with reference electrodes, and Faradaic (intercalation) and non-Faradaic (adsorption) processes in single electrode materials. While physical models are applied to well-known systems (Chapters \ref{chap:LTP}-\ref{chap:capacitor}), the work does not attempt comprehensive mechanistic modeling or systematic correlation of impedance features to specific battery degradation modes (Channle \ref{chap:coins}). Such applications require the foundation that this work provides: a robust, accessible platform for generating the large datasets needed for data-driven electrochemical and battery studies.

% Thesis outline
This thesis is organized as follows. Part I provides the theoretical framework: Chapter \ref{ch:electrochemistry} reviews fundamental electrochemistry concepts, Chapter \ref{ch:batteries} discusses battery technology and characterization methods, Chapter \ref{ch:system_identification} introduces system identification theory for frequency-domain characterization, Chapter \ref{ch:digital_signal_proc} and Chapter \ref{ch:deis state art} surveys the scientific literature state-of-the-art in non-stationary impedance spectroscopy.

Part II describes the methodologies developed in this work: Chapter \ref{ch:acquisition} details the automated data acquisition system, multi-sine signal design, and Chapter \ref{chap:estimation} the method for offline and online impedance estimation, while Chapter \ref{chap:analysis} presents the analysis approaches for parametric modeling of impedance data.

Part III reports results and discussion: Chapter \ref{chap:coins} validates the methodology on commercial coin cells throughout their cycling lifetime, Chapters \ref{chap:LTP}-\ref{chap:capacitor} demonstrate applications to single-electrode studies (lithium titanium phosphate and electrical double-layer capacitors), and Chapter \ref{chap:anodes} presents exploratory measurements on lithium and sodium metal anodes that reveal methodological challenges for future development. Chapters \ref{ch:conclusions}-\ref{ch:outlook} concludes with a summary of achievements and outlook for future work.
