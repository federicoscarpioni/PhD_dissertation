Electrochemistry provides the fundamental framework for understanding the operation and ageing of rechargeable batteries. 
All essential processes in a battery (ion intercalation, charge transfer, solid-electrolyte interphase formation, and lithium plating) are governed by electrochemical thermodynamics and kinetics of the redox reactions occurring at the interfaces between electrodes and electrolyte. 
The voltage, efficiency, power capability, and long-term stability of a cell are arise from these reactions and transport mechanisms taking place at these interfaces.

This chapter lays the foundation to understand basic electrochemical processes and how the appear in the impedance measurment for the characterization of batteries.

\section{Thermodynamics}

Electrochemical systems convert chemical energy into electrical work through redox reactions occurring at electrode-electrolyte interfaces. 
At equilibrium, when no net current flows in the cell, the driving force for electron transfer is quantified by the \emph{electrode potential}, which originates from differences in the electrochemical potential of ionic and electronic species in the two phases.

\subsection{Electrochemical potential}

For a species $i$ with charge number $z_i$, the electrochemical potential is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mu}_i = \mu_i + z_i F \phi,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential, $F$ is Faraday's constant, and $\phi$ is the electric potential. 
At thermodynamic equilibrium, the electrochemical potentials of all species involved in a half-reaction must be equal on both sides of the interface. 
For a generic half-reaction
\[
\sum_j \nu_j X_j + n e^- \rightleftharpoons \sum_k \nu_k X_k,
\]
the associated change in Gibbs free energy is related to the equilibrium electrode potential $E$ through
\begin{equation}
    \Delta G = -n F E,
\end{equation}
which provides the thermodynamic link between chemical composition and measurable cell voltage.

\subsection{The Nernst equation}

When the activities of the reacting species deviate from standard equilibrium conditions, the electrode potential shifts according to the Nernst equation:
\begin{equation}
    E = E^\circ + \frac{RT}{nF}
    \ln\left(
        \frac{\prod_k a_k^{\nu_k}}{\prod_j a_j^{\nu_j}}
    \right),
    \label{eq:nernst}
\end{equation}
where $E^\circ$ is the standard redox potential and $a_i$ is the activity of species $i$. Equation~\eqref{eq:nernst} shows that the electrode potential is determined not only by the intrinsic thermodynamics of the redox couple but also by the composition and temperature of the interface. 

\subsection{Measurement of electrode potentials}

Only potential differences can be measured experimentally. 
Therefore, determining the potential of an electrode requires a second electrode with a known and stable potential value. 
Hystorycally the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) was used to define the zero of the electrochemical reduction potential scale, but practical measurements typically rely on secondary references such as Ag/AgCl or calomel electrodes, which offer adequate stability with a much easier construction and maintenance. 
To keep the potential stable, the reference electrode must remain unaltered under small current drawn by the electrochemical instrumentation.

In systems where implementation of a classical reference electrode is not feasible, such as full batteries, a pseudo-reference can be realised by immersing a metal wire directly into the electrolyte. Although convenient, the potential of a pseudo-reference depends on the local ionic composition and often drifts during operation. 
Its use is acceptable only when the resulting uncertainty is quantified and compatible with the intended measurements.

A third option is the use of a quasi-reference electrode that is similar to a pseudo-reference in the sense that the electrode is immersed directly into the electrolyte solution without ionic separation, but it is constituted by a metal covered by its halogen forming a solid state redox couple system for better potential stability.

Accurate knowledge of the electrode potential is essential not only for thermodynamic characterisation but also for interpreting electrochemical impedance spectra. 
Because impedance measurements rely on small perturbations applied around a defined operating point, an uncertainty in the underlying equilibrium potential directly affects the meaning and reproducibility of the response.

\section{Kinetics}

While thermodynamics defines the equilibrium potential of an electrode, practical electrochemical systems operate away from equilibrium. 
When a current flows, the interfacial concentrations of reacting species deviate from their equilibrium values, leading to a measurable shift in potential known as the overpotential. 
Electrode kinetics provides a quantitative description of how charge transfer, mass transport, and interfacial phenomena contribute to this deviation.

\subsection{Overpotential and activation kinetics}

The overpotential $\eta$ is defined as the difference between the actual electrode potential and its equilibrium value,
\begin{equation}
    \eta = E - E_{\mathrm{eq}}.
\end{equation}
A positive overpotential accelerates oxidation, whereas a negative overpotential accelerates reduction. 
For small departures from equilibrium, the current is limited by the intrinsic kinetics of electron transfer between the electrode and the redox-active species. 
At sufficiently large overpotentials, mass transport may become rate-limiting.

\subsection{The Butler-Volmer equation}

The activation-controlled charge transfer is described by the Butler-Volmer equation,
\begin{equation}
    i = i_0 \left[ \exp\!\left( \frac{\alpha_a F \eta}{RT} \right) - \exp\!\left( -\frac{\alpha_c F \eta}{RT} \right) \right],
    \label{eq:BV}
\end{equation}
where $i_0$ is the exchange current density and $\alpha_a$ and $\alpha_c$ are the anodic and cathodic transfer coefficients. 
The exchange current density quantifies the intrinsic reactivity of the interface and depends on the concentrations of reacting species at the surface. 
Equation~\eqref{eq:BV} connects the near-equilibrium regime, where the response is linear, with the high-overpotential regime, where one exponential term dominates.
This equation highlight the intrinsic non-linearity of electrochemical systems, especially out of equilibrium; an important point for the following discussion of non-stationary Impedance Spectroscopy.

\subsection{Mass transport: diffusion and migration}

Charge-transfer kinetics alone cannot describe the behaviour of an electrochemical interface under operating conditions. 
When a net current flows, the interfacial concentrations of reacting species deviate from their bulk values, and mass transport determines how quickly these deviations are restored. 
The flux of an ionic species $i$ in solution is governed by the Nernst--Planck equation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{J}_i = -D_i \nabla c_i - z_i u_i F c_i \nabla \phi + c_i \mathbf{v},
    \label{eq:nernst-planck}
\end{equation}
where $D_i$ is the diffusion coefficient, $u_i$ the ionic mobility, $z_i$ the charge number, $c_i$ the concentration, $\phi$ the electric potential and $\mathbf{v}$ the solvent velocity. 
The three terms represent diffusion, migration in an electric field and convection, respectively. 
In typical laboratory electrochemical cells, convection is absent or deliberately suppressed, so $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.

The time evolution of concentration follows from the continuity equation,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial t} = -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}_i,
    \label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
which, in the absence of migration, reduces to Fick's second law:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial t} = D_i \nabla^2 c_i.
    \label{eq:ficks-law}
\end{equation}
Diffusion describes the spontaneous relaxation of concentration gradients, and the associated characteristic time scale is proportional to the square of the diffusion length. 
This slow relaxation becomes relevant whenever the rate of the electrochemical reaction is high enough to deplete or enrich the surface concentration relative to the bulk.

The migration term in Eq.~\eqref{eq:nernst-planck} accounts for the contribution of the electric field to ionic motion. 
Although migration is essential for maintaining electroneutrality on macroscopic length scales, its effect is strongly damped in regions of high ionic strength, such as battery electrolytes, where screening is efficient and concentration gradients dominate the local dynamics. 
Nevertheless, under conditions of significant current flow or low ionic conductivity, migration modifies the local distribution of ions and influences the effective transport parameters near the interface.

\subsection{Mass-transfer limitations and diffusion-controlled kinetics}

The Butler--Volmer equation assumes that the concentrations of reacting species at the electrode surface remain equal to their bulk values. 
When the reaction rate is high compared to the rate at which diffusion can replenish or remove reactive species, concentration gradients develop near the interface. 
This leads to a maximum sustainable current, known as the limiting current density,
\begin{equation}
    i_{\mathrm{lim}} = n F k_m c_{\infty},
\end{equation}
where $k_m$ is the mass-transfer coefficient and $c_{\infty}$ is the bulk concentration of the reactant. 
As the surface concentration approaches zero (for reduction) or saturation (for oxidation), the current no longer increases with overpotential.

In the presence of mass-transfer effects, the kinetic expression must combine both activation and diffusion contributions. 
A commonly used form is
\begin{equation}
    i = \left[ \frac{1}{i_{\mathrm{act}}} + \frac{1}{i_{\mathrm{diff}}} \right]^{-1},
    \label{eq:mixed-control}
\end{equation}
where $i_{\mathrm{act}}$ is the activation current given by Eq.~\eqref{eq:BV} and $i_{\mathrm{diff}}$ accounts for diffusive transport, typically written as
\begin{equation}
    i_{\mathrm{diff}} = i_{\mathrm{lim}} \left(1 - \frac{c_s}{c_{\infty}}\right).
\end{equation}
In steady-state diffusion-limited conditions, $i \to i_{\mathrm{lim}}$ regardless of how large $\eta$ becomes. 
In transient techniques such as impedance spectroscopy, mass transfer contributes a frequency-dependent impedance, commonly represented by the Warburg element. 
The onset of mass-transfer limitation therefore marks the transition from purely kinetic behaviour to mixed kinetic-diffusion behaviour, with characteristic signatures in the impedance spectrum.

\section{Double-layer structure and interfacial capacitance}

At the interface between an electronic conductor and an ionic solution, a separation of charge naturally develops. Electrons in the electrode and ions in the electrolyte arrange themselves such that the electrochemical potentials of all species are equal across the interface. The resulting region, known as the electrical double layer, stores charge electrostatically and therefore contributes a capacitive component to the interfacial impedance.

The double layer is commonly described as consisting of a compact region adjacent to the electrode surface and a diffuse region that extends into the electrolyte. In the compact layer, often referred to as the Helmholtz layer, specifically adsorbed ions and oriented solvent molecules form a thin dielectric layer of thickness on the order of molecular dimensions. Its capacitance per unit area can be approximated by that of a parallel-plate capacitor,
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{H}} \approx \frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{H}} \varepsilon_0}{d_{\mathrm{H}}},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{H}}$ is the effective dielectric constant of the compact layer and $d_{\mathrm{H}}$ its thickness.

Beyond the compact layer, ions distribute according to the balance of electrostatic and entropic forces. Assuming a symmetric $z:z$ electrolyte and a planar interface, the ion concentrations follow Boltzmann statistics, which combined with Poisson's equation lead to the Gouy--Chapman description of the diffuse region. The surface charge density $\sigma$ associated with a potential drop $\psi_0$ across the diffuse layer is then
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \sqrt{8 \varepsilon \varepsilon_0 R T c_\infty} \;
    \sinh\!\left( \frac{z F \psi_0}{2 R T} \right),
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ is the permittivity of the electrolyte, $c_\infty$ the bulk ion concentration and $z$ the ionic charge number. The corresponding differential capacitance is obtained by differentiation with respect to $\psi_0$,
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{GC}}(\psi_0) = \frac{d\sigma}{d\psi_0}
    = \frac{\varepsilon \varepsilon_0}{\lambda_D}
      \cosh\!\left( \frac{z F \psi_0}{2 R T} \right),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_D$ the Debye length,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon \varepsilon_0 R T}{2 z^2 F^2 c_\infty}}.
\end{equation}

The Stern model combines these two contributions. Since the compact and diffuse layers are arranged in series, the total differential double-layer capacitance is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{dl}}} = \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{H}}} + \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{GC}}(\psi_0)}.
\end{equation}
For highly concentrated electrolytes, as typically found in batteries, the Debye length becomes very small and the diffuse-layer capacitance grows correspondingly large, making the compact layer the dominant contributor to $C_{\mathrm{dl}}$. Conversely, in dilute electrolytes the diffuse layer significantly affects the interfacial response, and the potential dependence of $C_{\mathrm{GC}}$ can lead to pronounced nonlinearity.

\section{Electrochemical methods}

Electrochemical methods are broadly classified into direct current (DC) and alternating current (AC) techniques. This distinction highlights a crucial difference: DC methods typically probe macro-kinetic \footnote{overall, measured reaction rate that accounts for all physical steps (charge transfer, diffusion, ohmic losses) at the scale of the entire electrode} and thermodynamic parameters under large perturbations, while AC methods resolve specific interfacial mechanisms under small-signal control.

\subsection{DC Methods: Macro-Kinetics and Device Performance}
Direct current (DC) methods involve applying a constant or time-varying potential or current to the cell.

\subsubsection{Cyclic Voltammetry (CV)}
Historically, Cyclic Voltammetry (CV)—a potential sweeping method—has been fundamental in electroanalytical chemistry, mapping the thermodynamic potential window of redox species and characterizing chemical reversibility. In battery science, it is used to identify phase transitions and the potentials at which side reactions occur.

\subsubsection{Potentiostatic and Galvanostatic Methods}
Potentiostatic methods apply a constant potential (voltage). This approach fixes the **thermodynamic driving force** (the overpotential, $\eta$) and allows the resulting current response to be solely governed by the rate-limiting kinetic and transport processes. This is ideal for studying reaction mechanisms where the interfacial potential needs to be tightly controlled. In contrast, Galvanostatic methods apply a constant current (load). This is the standard method for simulating battery operation, yielding data for device-relevant metrics such as capacity, energy density, and cycle life, rather than isolated fundamental kinetic parameters.

\subsection{AC Methods: Small-Signal Dynamic Analysis}
The inability of DC methods to separate fundamental interfacial contributions (such as ohmic resistance, charge-transfer resistance, and double-layer capacitance) necessitated the development of alternating current (AC) techniques.

% \subsubsection{Historical Development and the Electrified Double Layer}
% The use of AC signals originated from the need to resolve the **Electrified Double Layer (EDL)**. Early work, dating back to Kohlrausch, showed that by using high-frequency AC signals, the large interfacial capacitance could be "short-circuited," allowing the isolation of the **pure ohmic resistance** of the electrolyte. Crucially, the frequency-dependent measurements of capacitance provided direct experimental evidence for the EDL, demonstrating that AC techniques are unique in their ability to resolve fundamental physical elements based on their distinct **time constants** ($\tau = RC$).

\subsubsection{Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS)}
Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) employs a small-amplitude, sinusoidal perturbation (typically $\pm 5-10 \text{ mV}$) applied over a wide range of frequencies. This small-signal approach ensures that the electrode remains in the **linear kinetic regime** and is only minimally perturbed from its DC operating point, which is itself defined by the cell's thermodynamics (SOC). This is the essential condition for applying the principles of linear circuit theory. Therefore, EIS does not discard thermodynamics; rather, it quantifies the **dynamic (kinetic and transport) response** of the system \emph{around} a defined thermodynamic state. The primary advantage is the ability to separate and quantify phenomena occurring at vastly different time scales (e.g., charge-transfer kinetics in milliseconds vs. solid-state diffusion in seconds).

\subsection{Three-Electrode Cell Configurations}
Accurate measurement of the overpotential generated by current flow is essential for interpreting both DC and AC data. While a simple two-electrode setup measures the potential difference across the entire cell, a three-electrode configuration is required to isolate the potential of the specific Working Electrode (WE) under study. 
This setup includes the WE, a Counter (Auxiliary) Electrode (CE) to carry the faradaic current, and a high-impedance Reference Electrode (RE) which measures the potential difference between the WE and the RE while drawing negligible current. The physical placement of the RE is critical. If positioned poorly, the measurement will include a significant potential drop due to the ohmic resistance of the electrolyte between the WE and RE, known as the $IR$ drop. This drop is a major source of error and must be minimized by placing the RE as close as possible to the WE (but away from current lines) or corrected using high-frequency EIS measurements. The common use of pseudo-reference electrodes in non-aqueous battery cells also necessitates careful characterization and quantification of potential drift.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) probes the linear response of an electrochemical system to a small sinusoidal perturbation in potential or current. If the perturbation is sufficiently small, the system behaves linearly around its operating point, and the ratio of the complex voltage and current amplitudes defines the impedance,
\begin{equation}
    Z(\omega) = \frac{\tilde{E}(\omega)}{\tilde{I}(\omega)},
\end{equation}
where $\omega$ is the angular frequency. By varying the frequency over several decades, EIS resolves processes occurring on disparate time scales, from double-layer charging at high frequency to diffusion and slow interfacial transformations at low frequency.

In the simplest interfacial model, the impedance of an electrode under activation control consists of a charge-transfer resistance $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ in parallel with the double-layer capacitance $C_{\mathrm{dl}}$. At high frequencies, the capacitive branch dominates and the impedance becomes primarily imaginary; at low frequencies, the capacitive reactance becomes large and the impedance approaches $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$. Deviations from ideal capacitive behaviour, commonly observed in rough or porous electrodes, are often captured using a constant-phase element to represent distributed interfacial properties.

\subsection{Linearisation and charge-transfer resistance}

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy relies on applying a small perturbation $\delta \eta$ around a well-defined operating point. In this limit, the nonlinear Butler-Volmer expression may be linearised. Expanding Eq.~\eqref{eq:BV} to first order about $\eta = 0$ yields
\begin{equation}
    i \approx \left( \frac{\partial i}{\partial \eta} \right)_{\eta=0} \eta = \frac{nF i_0}{RT}\,\eta.
\end{equation}
The inverse of this proportionality defines the charge-transfer resistance,
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{ct}} = \frac{RT}{nF i_0},
\end{equation}
which represents the kinetic contribution to the interfacial impedance. In the absence of mass-transfer limitations, the small-signal impedance of an activation-controlled interface therefore contains a resistive contribution $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ in parallel with the capacitive behaviour of the double layer.

\subsection{Double-layer capacitance}
In the context of small-signal electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, the double layer behaves as an effective capacitor whose value reflects the local ionic environment and electrode potential. Together with the charge-transfer resistance arising from activation kinetics, it forms a parallel combination that constitutes the fundamental interfacial element in equivalent-circuit models. Deviations from ideal capacitive behaviour, often observed in porous or heterogeneous electrodes, manifest as constant-phase-element (CPE) behaviour, but the physical origin remains the distributed nature of interfacial charge storage and transport.

\end{equation}
depending on boundary condition.

\subsection{Impedance plots}

Magnitude and phase are
\begin{equation}
    |Z(\omega)| = \sqrt{ \big(\Re Z\big)^2 + \big(\Im Z\big)^2 },
    \qquad
    \phi(\omega) = \arctan\!\left( \frac{\Im Z}{\Re Z} \right).
    \label{eq:mag_phase}
\end{equation}
Nyquist: plot $-\Im(Z)$ vs $\Re(Z)$; each point corresponds to a frequency. Bode: plot $|Z(\omega)|$ and $\phi(\omega)$ vs $\omega$; useful for separating overlapping processes by corner frequencies.

\subsection{Data quality and validation}

Causality, linearity, and stability imply Kramers-Kronig (KK) consistency. Report KK residuals or use H-/J-tests; large violations indicate drift, nonlinearity, or artifacts. Verify linearity by checking that $Z$ does not depend on perturbation amplitude and by absence of harmonic distortion. Use coherence or repeatability to justify fit weights and average multiple records when possible.

Uncompensated $IR$ and RE placement matter: minimise $R_s$ contributions to the measured overpotential or correct them post hoc. Temperature strongly affects impedance; monitor/control $T$.

\subsection{Diffusion impedance}

When mass transport contributes to the interfacial response, the impedance acquires a characteristic frequency dependence that reflects the dynamics of concentration relaxation. Starting from the diffusion equation under small-signal perturbation,
\[
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D \nabla^2 c,
\]
a sinusoidal perturbation of frequency $\omega$ leads to concentration profiles that vary as $\exp(-\sqrt{j\omega/D}\, x)$. The associated flux at the surface is therefore proportional to $(j\omega)^{1/2}$, giving rise to the classical diffusion impedance.

\subsubsection{Semi-infinite (infinite-length) Warburg impedance}

For a planar electrode with a semi-infinite diffusion domain, the small-signal diffusion impedance is
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\mathrm{W}}(\omega) = \sigma_{\mathrm{W}} \frac{1 - j}{\sqrt{\omega}},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\mathrm{W}}$ is the Warburg coefficient. The real and imaginary parts are equal, producing a line at $45^\circ$ in the complex plane. This form arises when the perturbation penetrates only a small fraction of the electrolyte or electrode, such that the concentration field does not reach a physical boundary during the oscillation period.

\subsubsection{Finite-length Warburg impedance}

When diffusion occurs in a bounded domain—such as within porous electrodes, solid diffusion in active particles, or electrolyte confined between electrodes—the diffusion layer cannot grow indefinitely. The impedance then takes one of two canonical forms depending on the boundary conditions.

For reflective (blocking) boundaries, the finite-length Warburg impedance is
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\mathrm{W,\,ref}}(\omega) = \sigma_{\mathrm{W}} \frac{\tanh\!\left(\sqrt{j\omega\tau_D}\right)}{\sqrt{j\omega}},
\end{equation}
while for transmissive (open) boundaries,
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\mathrm{W,\,open}}(\omega) = \sigma_{\mathrm{W}} \frac{\coth\!\left(\sqrt{j\omega\tau_D}\right)}{\sqrt{j\omega}},
\end{equation}
where $\tau_D = L^2/D$ is the characteristic diffusion time for a layer of thickness $L$. At high frequencies, both expressions recover the semi-infinite Warburg form, whereas at low frequencies the impedance transitions to a purely real resistance reflecting complete depletion of the diffusion layer.

Diffusion-related impedance elements are essential for interpreting battery electrode behaviour, where mass transport in the electrolyte, within porous electrode structures, and through solid particles strongly influences the dynamic response. The distinction between semi-infinite and finite-length diffusion is particularly important when analysing non-stationary or transient impedance measurements, as the effective diffusion domain may change with state of charge, cycling history, or electrode microstructure.

\section{Potentiostat and galvanostat architecture}

Modern electrochemical measurements rely on potentiostats or galvanostats, electronic instruments designed to control and measure the electrical variables of an electrochemical cell with high precision and bandwidth. Although commercial instruments vary in implementation, their internal structure is based on a small number of fundamental analogue building blocks that implement the control loops required for accurate electrochemical operation.

While seldom overlooked, the inner function of the instrument helps the scientist to design more appropriate experiment and troubleshoot problems. 
For this work insted, it is necessary to support the choice on the setup assembling discussed in Chapter \ref{ch:acquisition}

\subsection{Potentiostat operation}

The purpose of a potentiostat is to maintain the potential of a working electrode (WE) at a prescribed value relative to a reference electrode (RE). This is achieved using a high-gain feedback loop. The potential difference between WE and RE is measured through a high-impedance voltage follower connected to the RE. This stage ensures that virtually no current flows through the RE, preserving its equilibrium potential. The measured potential is compared to a user-defined setpoint, and the resulting error signal drives a power amplifier connected to the counter electrode (CE). By adjusting the CE potential, the amplifier forces the WE potential toward the setpoint. The output stage must source or sink the full cell current while maintaining stability of the feedback loop.

The current flowing through the WE-CE path is measured by a current-to-voltage converter, typically implemented using a transimpedance amplifier. The converter output provides a low-noise measurement of the cell current and is digitised for acquisition and further processing. The transimpedance gain determines the current range, and switching networks or programmable gain amplifiers are often used to accommodate multiple decades of current.

\subsection{Galvanostatic control}

In galvanostatic mode, the instrument controls the current rather than the potential. The measured current is compared to a desired setpoint, and the feedback loop adjusts the CE potential to maintain the prescribed current. The WE potential freely responds to the applied current and is measured through the high-impedance RE input. Internally, the same analogue blocks are used as in potentiostatic operation, but with the error signal derived from the difference between measured and commanded current. High-quality galvanostatic control requires wide bandwidth and accurate transimpedance conversion, as well as protection circuitry to prevent saturation when the cell dynamics change abruptly.

\subsection{Auxiliary analogue inputs and outputs}

Modern potentiostats incorporate auxiliary input and output channels to enable external excitation or synchronisation with additional hardware. Analogue output ports are typically connected upstream of the main control loop, allowing externally generated waveforms to be superimposed on the programmed potential or current. Depending on the instrument architecture, the auxiliary output is added either at the setpoint input of the control amplifier or directly at the summing node of the error amplifier. This functionality is essential for experiments requiring composite perturbations, such as multisine excitation superimposed on galvanostatic cycling.

Auxiliary analogue inputs provide direct access to the measured analogue signals before digital conversion. These inputs may tap into the potential follower, the current-to-voltage converter, or other diagnostic nodes within the instrument. Recording these signals enables high-resolution acquisition beyond the default sampling configuration of the potentiostat and facilitates the development of custom measurement protocols, including broadband impedance excitation and time-resolved analysis. Because the auxiliary ports bypass part of the instrument's internal filtering and signal conditioning, their bandwidth is often significantly higher than that of the standard measurement channel, making them suitable for external oscilloscopes or dedicated data-acquisition systems.

\subsection{Digital control and communication}

Although the analogue front-end implements the critical control functions, contemporary potentiostats operate under digital supervision. A microcontroller or embedded processor manages range selection, filter configuration, calibration, safety interlocks, and communication with a host computer. The digital unit also handles data transfer, typically via USB, Ethernet or proprietary interfaces, and provides synchronization mechanisms for combined measurements with external equipment. From the user's perspective the potentiostat behaves as a stand-alone device, but internally it exchanges commands and measurement data continuously with the host software, which orchestrates the experiment, logs data, and provides real-time visualisation.

The combination of analogue control loops, high-resolution converters, and versatile auxiliary interfaces makes modern potentiostats suitable platforms for advanced perturbation techniques. In particular, the capability to superimpose external excitation signals and to record high-bandwidth analogue responses is crucial for non-stationary broadband impedance measurements, as developed in this work.
