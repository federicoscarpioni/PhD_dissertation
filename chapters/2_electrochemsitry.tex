\chapter{Electrochemistry}
% \minitoc

Electrochemistry provides the fundamental framework for understanding the operation and ageing of rechargeable batteries. 
All essential processes in a battery (ion intercalation, charge transfer, solid-electrolyte interphase formation, and lithium plating) are governed by electrochemical thermodynamics and kinetics of the redox reactions occurring at the interfaces between electrodes and electrolyte. 
The voltage, efficiency, power capability, and long-term stability of a cell arise from these reactions and transport mechanisms taking place at these interfaces.

To characterize these processes in electrochemistry, the Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) technique is used for its capability of separating processes occurring at different time scales in one single experiment while being non-destructive to the system and operating \emph{in situ}, meaning that the system is kept in its operational state (in contrast to other spectroscopy analyses that require specific sample preparation). 

EIS does not probe isolated physicochemical phenomena, but rather the linearized response of a coupled system involving charge transfer, mass transport, and interfacial storage.
A meaningful interpretation of impedance spectra therefore requires identifying which electrochemical processes can contribute to the measured response, over which time and length scales, and under which thermodynamic and kinetic assumptions.

For this reason, the present chapter introduces only those electrochemical concepts that directly govern the small-signal response of an electrochemical interface, with particular emphasis on interfacial kinetics, capacitive storage, and transport phenomena that manifest as distinct features in the impedance spectrum. 
First, the thermodynamics and kinetics of interfacial redox reactions are discussed, referring to the basic equations describing electrode potential and charge-transfer resistance.
Then, the structure of the electrical double-layer is described, including the adsorption capacitance.
The chapter follows with a brief description of electrochemical methods based on direct or alternating current control.

\section{Thermodynamics}

Electrochemical systems convert chemical energy into electrical work through redox reactions occurring at electrode-electrolyte interfaces. 
At equilibrium, when no net current flows in the cell, the driving force for electron transfer is quantified by the \emph{electrode potential}, which originates from differences in the electrochemical potential of ionic and electronic species in the two phases.

\subsection{Electrochemical potential}

For a species $i$ with charge number $z_i$, the electrochemical potential is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mu}_i = \mu_i + z_i F \phi,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential, $F$ is Faraday's constant, and $\phi$ is the electric potential. 
At thermodynamic equilibrium, the electrochemical potentials of all species involved in a half-reaction must be equal on both sides of the interface. 
For a generic half-reaction
\[
\sum_j \nu_j X_j + n e^- \rightleftharpoons \sum_k \nu_k X_k,
\]
the associated change in Gibbs free energy is related to the equilibrium electrode potential $E$ through
\begin{equation}
    \Delta G = -n F E,
\end{equation}
which provides the thermodynamic link between chemical composition and measurable cell voltage.

\subsection{The Nernst equation}

When the activities of the reacting species deviate from standard equilibrium conditions, the electrode potential shifts according to the Nernst equation:
\begin{equation}
    E = E^\circ + \frac{RT}{nF}
    \ln\left(
        \frac{\prod_k a_k^{\nu_k}}{\prod_j a_j^{\nu_j}}
    \right),
    \label{eq:nernst}
\end{equation}
where $E^\circ$ is the standard redox potential and $a_i$ is the activity of species $i$. Equation \ref{eq:nernst} shows that the electrode potential is determined not only by the intrinsic thermodynamics of the redox couple but also by the composition and temperature of the interface. 

\subsection{Measurement of electrode potentials}

Only potential differences can be measured experimentally. 
Therefore, determining the potential of an electrode requires a second electrode with a known and stable potential value. 
Historically, the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) was used to define the zero of the electrochemical reduction potential scale, but practical measurements typically rely on secondary references such as Ag/AgCl or calomel electrodes, which offer adequate stability with simpler construction and maintenance. 
To keep the potential stable, the reference electrode must remain unaltered under small current drawn by the electrochemical instrumentation.

In systems where a classical reference electrode cannot be implemented, such as in full battery cells, a pseudo-reference can be realized by immersing a metal wire directly into the electrolyte. Although convenient, the potential of a pseudo-reference depends on the local ionic composition and often drifts during operation. 
Its use is acceptable only when the resulting uncertainty is quantified and compatible with the intended measurements.

A third option is the use of a quasi-reference electrode that is similar to a pseudo-reference in the sense that the electrode is immersed directly into the electrolyte solution without ionic separation, but it is constituted by a metal covered by its halogen forming a solid state redox couple system for better potential stability.

Accurate knowledge of the electrode potential is essential not only for thermodynamic characterization but also for interpreting electrochemical impedance spectra. 
Because impedance measurements rely on small perturbations applied around a defined operating point, an uncertainty in the underlying equilibrium potential directly affects the meaning and reproducibility of the response.

\section{Kinetics}

While thermodynamics defines the equilibrium potential of an electrode, practical electrochemical systems operate away from equilibrium. 
When a current flows, the interfacial concentrations of reacting species deviate from their equilibrium values, leading to a measurable shift in potential known as the overpotential. 
Electrode kinetics provides a quantitative description of how charge transfer, mass transport, and interfacial phenomena contribute to this deviation.

\subsection{Overpotential and activation kinetics}

The overpotential $\eta$ is defined as the difference between the actual electrode potential and its equilibrium value,
\begin{equation}
    \eta = E - E_{\mathrm{eq}}.
\end{equation}
A positive overpotential accelerates oxidation, whereas a negative overpotential accelerates reduction. 
For small departures from equilibrium, the current is limited by the intrinsic kinetics of electron transfer between the electrode and the redox-active species. 
At sufficiently large overpotentials, mass transport may become rate-limiting.

\subsection{The Butler-Volmer equation}

The activation-controlled charge transfer is described by the Butler-Volmer equation,
\begin{equation}
    i = i_0 \left[ \exp\!\left( \frac{\alpha_a F \eta}{RT} \right) - \exp\!\left( -\frac{\alpha_c F \eta}{RT} \right) \right],
    \label{eq:BV}
\end{equation}
where $i_0$ is the exchange current density and $\alpha_a$ and $\alpha_c$ are the anodic and cathodic transfer coefficients. 
The exchange current density quantifies the intrinsic reactivity of the interface and depends on the concentrations of reacting species at the surface. 
Equation \ref{eq:BV} connects the near-equilibrium regime, where the response is linear, with the high-overpotential regime, where one exponential term dominates.
This equation highlights the intrinsic non-linearity of electrochemical systems, especially far from equilibrium; an important point for the following discussion of non-stationary impedance spectroscopy.

\subsection{Mass transport: diffusion and migration}

Charge-transfer kinetics alone cannot describe the behavior of an electrochemical interface under operating conditions. 
When a net current flows, the interfacial concentrations of reacting species deviate from their bulk values, and mass transport determines how quickly these deviations are restored. 
The flux of an ionic species $i$ in solution is governed by the Nernst--Planck equation,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{J}_i = -D_i \nabla c_i - z_i u_i F c_i \nabla \phi + c_i \mathbf{v},
    \label{eq:nernst-planck}
\end{equation}
where $D_i$ is the diffusion coefficient, $u_i$ the ionic mobility, $z_i$ the charge number, $c_i$ the concentration, $\phi$ the electric potential and $\mathbf{v}$ the solvent velocity. 
The three terms represent diffusion, migration in an electric field and convection, respectively. 
In typical laboratory electrochemical cells, convection is absent or deliberately suppressed, so $\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0}$.

The time evolution of concentration follows from the continuity equation,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial t} = -\nabla \cdot \mathbf{J}_i,
    \label{eq:continuity}
\end{equation}
which, in the absence of migration, reduces to Fick's second law:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c_i}{\partial t} = D_i \nabla^2 c_i.
    \label{eq:ficks-law}
\end{equation}
Diffusion describes the spontaneous relaxation of concentration gradients, and the associated characteristic time scale is proportional to the square of the diffusion length. 
This slow relaxation becomes relevant whenever the rate of the electrochemical reaction is high enough to deplete or enrich the surface concentration relative to the bulk.

The migration term in Eq. \ref{eq:nernst-planck} accounts for the contribution of the electric field to ionic motion. 
Although migration is essential for maintaining electroneutrality on macroscopic length scales, its effect is strongly damped in regions of high ionic strength, such as battery electrolytes, where screening is efficient and concentration gradients dominate the local dynamics. 
Nevertheless, under conditions of significant current flow or low ionic conductivity, migration modifies the local distribution of ions and influences the effective transport parameters near the interface.

\subsection{Mass-transfer limitations and diffusion-controlled kinetics}

The Butler--Volmer equation assumes that the concentrations of reacting species at the electrode surface remain equal to their bulk values. 
When the reaction rate is high compared to the rate at which diffusion can replenish or remove reactive species, concentration gradients develop near the interface. 
This leads to a maximum sustainable current, known as the limiting current density,
\begin{equation}
    i_{\mathrm{lim}} = n F k_m c_{\infty},
\end{equation}
where $k_m$ is the mass-transfer coefficient and $c_{\infty}$ is the bulk concentration of the reactant. 
As the surface concentration approaches zero (for reduction) or saturation (for oxidation), the current no longer increases with overpotential.

In the presence of mass-transfer effects, the kinetic expression must combine both activation and diffusion contributions. 
A commonly used form is
\begin{equation}
    i = \left[ \frac{1}{i_{\mathrm{act}}} + \frac{1}{i_{\mathrm{diff}}} \right]^{-1},
    \label{eq:mixed-control}
\end{equation}
where $i_{\mathrm{act}}$ is the activation current given by Eq. \ref{eq:BV} and $i_{\mathrm{diff}}$ accounts for diffusive transport, typically written as
\begin{equation}
    i_{\mathrm{diff}} = i_{\mathrm{lim}} \left(1 - \frac{c_s}{c_{\infty}}\right).
\end{equation}
In steady-state diffusion-limited conditions, $i \to i_{\mathrm{lim}}$ regardless of how large $\eta$ becomes. 

Figure \ref{fig:bulter-volmer} illustrates the nonlinear current--overpotential relationship described by the Butler--Volmer equation, highlighting both the diffusion-limited regime and the near-equilibrium region where linearization is valid. The latter regime forms the basis of impedance spectroscopy, as small perturbations around the operating point lead to a proportional relationship between current and overpotential.

\section{Double-layer structure and interfacial capacitance}

At the interface between an electronic conductor and an ionic solution, a separation of charge develops. 
Electrons in the electrode and ions in the electrolyte arrange themselves such that the electrochemical potentials of all species are equal across the interface. 
The resulting region, known as the electrical double layer, stores charge electrostatically and therefore contributes a capacitive component to the interfacial impedance.

The double layer is commonly described as consisting of a compact region adjacent to the electrode surface and a diffuse region that extends into the electrolyte. 
In the compact layer, often referred to as the Helmholtz layer, specifically adsorbed ions and oriented solvent molecules form a thin dielectric layer of thickness on the order of molecular dimensions (Ångstrom). 
Its capacitance per unit area can be approximated by that of a parallel-plate capacitor,
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{H}} \approx \frac{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{H}} \varepsilon_0}{d_{\mathrm{H}}},
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon_{\mathrm{H}}$ is the effective dielectric constant of the compact layer and $d_{\mathrm{H}}$ its thickness.

Beyond the compact layer, ions distribute according to the balance of electrostatic and entropic forces.
Assuming a symmetric $z:z$ electrolyte and a planar interface, the ion concentrations follow Boltzmann statistics, which combined with Poisson's equation lead to the Gouy--Chapman description of the diffuse region. 
The surface charge density $\sigma$ associated with a potential drop $\psi_0$ across the diffuse layer is then
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \sqrt{8 \varepsilon \varepsilon_0 R T c_\infty} \;
    \sinh\!\left( \frac{z F \psi_0}{2 R T} \right),
\end{equation}
where $\varepsilon$ is the permittivity of the electrolyte, $c_\infty$ the bulk ion concentration and $z$ the ionic charge number. 
The corresponding differential capacitance is obtained by differentiation with respect to $\psi_0$,
\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{GC}}(\psi_0) = \frac{d\sigma}{d\psi_0}
    = \frac{\varepsilon \varepsilon_0}{\lambda_D}
      \cosh\!\left( \frac{z F \psi_0}{2 R T} \right),
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_D$ the Debye length,
\begin{equation}
    \lambda_D = \sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon \varepsilon_0 R T}{2 z^2 F^2 c_\infty}}.
\end{equation}

The Stern model combines these two contributions. 
Since the compact and diffuse layers are arranged in series, the total differential double-layer capacitance is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{dl}}} = \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{H}}} + \frac{1}{C_{\mathrm{GC}}(\psi_0)}.
\end{equation}
For highly concentrated electrolytes, as typically found in batteries, the Debye length becomes very small and the diffuse-layer capacitance grows correspondingly large, making the compact layer the dominant contributor to $C_{\mathrm{dl}}$ \cite{smithElectrostaticScreeningLength2016}.
Conversely, in dilute electrolytes the diffuse layer significantly affects the interfacial response, and the potential dependence of $C_{\mathrm{GC}}$ can lead to pronounced nonlinearity.

\section{Electrochemical methods}

Electrochemical methods are broadly classified into direct current (DC) and alternating current (AC) techniques. 
This distinction highlights a crucial difference: DC methods typically probe macro-kinetic \footnote{overall, measured reaction rate that accounts for all physical steps (charge transfer, diffusion, ohmic losses) at the scale of the entire electrode} and thermodynamic parameters under large perturbations, while AC methods resolve specific interfacial mechanisms under small-signal control (perturbative).

\subsection{DC methods}

Direct current (DC) methods involve applying a constant or time-varying potential or current to the cell.

Historically, cyclic voltammetry (CV) has been fundamental in electroanalytical chemistry, probing the thermodynamic potential window of redox species and characterizing chemical reversibility. 

An alternative approach to voltage-controlled techniques is the potentiostatic method, in which the electrode potential is held constant. 
This approach fixes the \emph{thermodynamic driving force} (the overpotential, $\eta$) and allows the resulting current response to be solely governed by the rate-limiting kinetic and transport processes.
It is used, for example, in electrolyzers or fuel cells.
The current output varies depending on the interface electrochemistry so the methods are also called chrono-amperometry. 
This is ideal for studying reaction mechanisms where the interfacial potential needs to be tightly controlled. 

In contrast, galvanostatic methods apply a constant current to the cell. 
This is the standard method for simulating battery operation, yielding data for device-relevant metrics such as capacity, energy density, and cycle life, rather than isolated fundamental kinetic parameters.

\subsection{AC methods}

The use of AC signals originated from the need to resolve the electrified double-layer. 
Early work, dating back to Kohlrausch, showed that by using high-frequency AC signals, the large interfacial capacitance could be \"short-circuited\", allowing the isolation of the pure ohmic resistance of the electrolyte. 
Crucially, the frequency-dependent measurements of capacitance provided direct experimental evidence for the electrical double-layer, demonstrating that AC techniques are unique in their ability to resolve fundamental physical elements based on their distinct \emph{time constants} ($\tau = RC$).

\subsubsection{Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy}
\label{sec:EIS}

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is a non-destructive technique that employs a small-amplitude sinusoidal perturbation (typically $\pm 5-10 \text{ mV}$) applied over a wide range of frequencies. 
The impedance is the frequency dependent function:
\begin{equation}
    Z(\omega) = \frac{\tilde{E}(\omega)}{\tilde{I}(\omega)},
\end{equation}
where $\omega$ is the angular frequency.
The tilde notation over $\tilde{E}$ and $\tilde{I}$ denotes the phasor representation proposed by Orazem and Tribollet \cite{orazemEIS}.
By varying the frequency of the sinusoidal perturbation, the impedance response of different interfacial processes is measured; hence the name spectroscopy.
The primary advantage of impedance spectroscopy is its ability to separate and quantify phenomena occurring at vastly different time scales without altering the system state.

In the simplest interfacial model, the impedance of an electrode under activation control consists of a charge-transfer resistance $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ in parallel with the double-layer capacitance $C_{\mathrm{dl}}$. 
At high frequencies, the capacitive branch dominates and the impedance becomes primarily imaginary.
At low frequencies, the capacitive reactance becomes large and the impedance approaches $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$. 
Deviations from ideal capacitive behavior, commonly observed in rough or porous electrodes, are captured using a constant-phase element to represent distributed interfacial properties.
The representation of the interface as charge-transfer resistance $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ and double-layer capacitance $C_{\mathrm{dl}}$ is obtained analytically from the linearization of the Butler-Volmer equation and the double-layer capacitance of the Stern model.

Before the advent of microcomputers in the 70s, impedance was estimated frequency by frequency manually with the aid of an analog circuit called lock-in amplifier.
The impedance $Z(\omega_k)$ is calculated from the amplitude ratio and phase shift $\Delta\phi$ between input (reference) and output signals using the formula 
\begin{equation}
Z(\omega_k) = \frac{A_I}{A_O}e^{j\omega\Delta\phi}.
\end{equation}

Later, digital signal processing was integrated into micro-controllers, creating the digital Frequency Response Analyzer (FRA) capable of automatic frequency sweeping, averaging, automatic current range selection, and application of voltage or current bias (Solartron 1174 in 1978 and Solartron 1250 in 1983). 
These instruments set the standard for impedance measurements that we still find today in commercial devices. 

\subsection{Linearisation and charge-transfer resistance}

In the limit of small perturbations, the nonlinear Butler-Volmer expression may be linearized by expanding Eq. \ref{eq:BV} to first order about $\eta = 0$, yielding:
\begin{equation}
    i \approx \left( \frac{\partial i}{\partial \eta} \right)_{\eta=0} \eta = \frac{nF i_0}{RT}\,\eta.
\end{equation}
The inverse of this proportionality defines the charge-transfer resistance,
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{ct}} = \frac{RT}{nF i_0},
\end{equation}
which represents the kinetic contribution to the interfacial impedance. 
In the absence of mass-transfer limitations, the small-signal impedance of an activation-controlled interface therefore contains a resistive contribution $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ in parallel with the capacitive behaviour of the double layer.

\subsection{Double-layer capacitance}

Under small perturbations, the double layer behaves as an effective capacitor whose value reflects the adsorbed charges and electrode potential. 
Together with the charge-transfer resistance arising from activation kinetics, it forms a parallel combination that constitutes the fundamental interfacial element in equivalent-circuit models. 

Deviations from ideal capacitive behaviour, often observed in porous or heterogeneous electrodes, manifest as constant-phase-element (CPE) behaviour, but the physical origin remains the distributed nature of interfacial charge storage and transport.

\subsection{Diffusion impedance}

When mass transport contributes to the interfacial response, the impedance acquires a characteristic frequency dependence that reflects the dynamics of concentration relaxation.
Starting from the diffusion equation under small-signal perturbation,
\[
\frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D \nabla^2 c,
\]
a sinusoidal perturbation of frequency $\omega$ leads to concentration profiles that vary as $\exp(-\sqrt{j\omega/D}\, x)$. The associated flux at the surface is therefore proportional to $(j\omega)^{1/2}$, giving rise to the classical diffusion impedance.

In the complex plane, diffusion impedance manifests as characteristic frequency-dependent trajectories whose shape reflects the underlying transport geometry as described in the rest of the section.

\subsubsection{Semi-infinite (infinite-length) Warburg impedance}

For a planar electrode with a semi-infinite diffusion domain, the small-signal diffusion impedance is
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\mathrm{W}}(\omega) = \sigma_{\mathrm{W}} \frac{1 - j}{\sqrt{\omega}},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{\mathrm{W}}$ is the Warburg coefficient. The real and imaginary parts are equal, producing a line at $45^\circ$ in the complex plane. This form arises when the perturbation penetrates only a small fraction of the electrolyte or electrode, such that the concentration field does not reach a physical boundary during the oscillation period.

\subsubsection{Finite-length Warburg impedance}

When diffusion occurs in a bounded domain—such as within porous electrodes, solid diffusion in active particles, or electrolyte confined between electrodes—the diffusion layer cannot grow indefinitely. The impedance then takes one of two canonical forms depending on the boundary conditions.

For reflective (blocking) boundaries, the finite-length Warburg impedance is
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\mathrm{W,\,ref}}(\omega) = \sigma_{\mathrm{W}} \frac{\tanh\!\left(\sqrt{j\omega\tau_D}\right)}{\sqrt{j\omega}},
\end{equation}
while for transmissive (open) boundaries,
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\mathrm{W,\,open}}(\omega) = \sigma_{\mathrm{W}} \frac{\coth\!\left(\sqrt{j\omega\tau_D}\right)}{\sqrt{j\omega}},
\end{equation}
where $\tau_D = L^2/D$ is the characteristic diffusion time for a layer of thickness $L$. At high frequencies, both expressions recover the semi-infinite Warburg form, whereas at low frequencies the impedance transitions to a purely real resistance reflecting complete depletion of the diffusion layer.

Diffusion-related impedance elements are essential for interpreting battery electrode behaviour, where mass transport in the electrolyte, within porous electrode structures, and through solid particles strongly influences the dynamic response. 
The distinction between semi-infinite and finite-length diffusion is particularly important when analyzing non-stationary or transient impedance measurements, as the effective diffusion domain may change with state of charge, cycling history, or electrode microstructure.

\subsection{Visualize impedance}

The complex impedance can be represented graphically in two equivalent forms: the Bode representation and the Nyquist representation.
In the Bode plot, the magnitude $|Z(\omega)|$ and phase $\phi(\omega)$ are displayed as functions of frequency on logarithmic axes. This format is particularly useful for identifying characteristic time constants and separating processes occurring over different frequency decades.

Alternatively, the Nyquist plot represents the negative imaginary part of the impedance as a function of its real part for each excitation frequency. Each point on the curve corresponds to a specific frequency, although frequency is not explicitly shown on the axes. For electrochemical systems, this representation is often preferred because it allows a direct geometric interpretation of the impedance. Ohmic resistances appear as intercepts on the real axis, while interfacial processes characterized by parallel resistance-capacitance elements produce semicircular arcs whose diameter corresponds to the associated resistance. Diffusion-controlled processes generate straight lines or curved tails at approximately $45^\circ$ or tending toward the vertical direction at low frequency.

For complex electrochemical systems containing multiple overlapping processes, the Nyquist representation provides an immediate visual assessment of the relative contributions of resistive and capacitive elements and of their evolution with operating conditions. For this reason, Nyquist plots are used extensively throughout this work to analyze time-varying impedance data.

When interpreting Nyquist plots geometrically, it is essential that the real and imaginary axes are plotted with identical scaling. Only with equal axis scaling do semicircles, straight diffusion lines, and intercepts retain their correct geometric meaning; any distortion of the aspect ratio can lead to misinterpretation of resistances and time constants.

\section{Equivalent circuit models}

The linearization of the electrochemical equations yields processes that behave as electrical elements connected in series or parallel, depending on the system characteristics.
To visualize the components, one typically translates the set of equations into a circuit and applies the laws of series and parallel connection of electrical elements to obtain a simplified and linearized model of the interface.
This approach was introduced by Randles \cite{randles1947kinetics} \cite{randles1952kinetics} and it is the \emph{de facto} method to visualize models in scientific publications.

\section{Validity and consistency of electrochemical impedance}

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy relies on a set of assumptions that define the conditions under which impedance is a physically meaningful quantity. 

First, the system must be \emph{linear} with respect to the applied perturbation. 
This requires that the amplitude of the AC excitation be sufficiently small so that the response can be accurately described by the first-order linearisation of the governing equations. 
In practice, this condition implies that the measured impedance must be independent of the perturbation amplitude and that higher harmonics in the response are negligible.

Second, the system must be \emph{stationary} during the measurement, meaning that the underlying thermodynamic state (electrode potential, surface composition, temperature, state of charge) does not change significantly over the duration of the impedance experiment. 
This assumption is often implicit in classical EIS but becomes critical in battery systems, where slow processes such as diffusion, phase transitions, and interphase growth continuously evolve.

Third, the system must be \emph{stable} and \emph{causal}. 
Stability ensures that bounded perturbations produce bounded responses, while causality requires that the response at any time depends only on present and past inputs. 
These properties are automatically satisfied for most passive electrochemical systems under equilibrium or near-equilibrium conditions, but may be violated in the presence of strong feedback, parasitic reactions, or measurement artefacts.

\subsection{Kramers--Kronig relations}

A fundamental consequence of linearity, causality, and stability is that the real and imaginary parts of the impedance are not independent. 
They are linked by the Kramers--Kronig (KK) relations, which express each component as a Hilbert transform of the other.

For an impedance function $Z(\omega) = Z'(\omega) + j Z''(\omega)$, the KK relations read
\begin{equation}
    Z'(\omega) = \frac{2}{\pi} \mathcal{P}
    \int_{0}^{\infty}
    \frac{\omega' Z''(\omega')}{\omega'^2 - \omega^2} \, d\omega',
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    Z''(\omega) = -\frac{2\omega}{\pi} \mathcal{P}
    \int_{0}^{\infty}
    \frac{Z'(\omega')}{\omega'^2 - \omega^2} \, d\omega',
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{P}$ denotes the Cauchy principal value.

In electrochemistry, the KK relations provide a powerful consistency check: 
if an experimentally measured impedance violates KK relations beyond experimental uncertainty, at least one of the underlying assumptions of EIS has been violated.

In practice, KK relations cannot be exactly verified by the above equation because they require spectral information from zero to infinite frequency.
Fitting experimental data to a circuit consisting of a series of RC elements produces a close approximation to the formula and it is usually referred as a KK test \cite{Boukamp_1995, orazemEIS,SCHONLEBER201420}.

From an experimental standpoint, deviations from KK consistency can arise from several sources: non-linearity or non-stationarity of the system, artifacts introduced by cell design, or the instrumentation electronics.
