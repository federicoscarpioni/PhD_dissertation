\chapter{Batteries}

Electrochemical energy storage systems convert chemical energy into electrical work through redox reactions occurring at electrode interfaces.
Among these systems, rechargeable (or secondary) batteries play a central role in portable electronics, stationary storage and electric mobility. 
Lithium-ion batteries, in particular, dominate current applications due to their high operating voltage, large specific energy and excellent cycle efficiency. 
This chapter introduces the fundamental principles of rechargeable batteries, their active materials, performance metrics and technological evolution. 
Although lithium-ion batteries serve as the primary experimental platform in this work, the discussion is formulated to remain broadly applicable.

The term \emph{battery} historically refers to a stack of electrochemical cells connected in series. 
In modern usage, it commonly denotes an electrochemical device for long-term energy storage, even when consisting of a single cell with two electrodes.

While lithium-ion batteries constitute the main experimental reference, the concepts presented in this chapter apply to rechargeable batteries based on different charge carriers, electrolytes and storage mechanisms.

Rather than providing an exhaustive review of specific electrode materials, this thesis adopts a mechanism-oriented perspective. 
Individual battery chemistries and materials are introduced only when required to interpret experimental results, particularly impedance measurements.

\section{Basic principles of operation}

A battery consists of two electrodes separated by an electrolyte that is electronically insulating and ionically conductive. 
During discharge, oxidation occurs at the negative electrode, releasing electrons to the external circuit and cations into the electrolyte. 
Simultaneously, reduction takes place at the positive electrode, where electrons arriving from the external circuit enable cation insertion or reaction at the interface. 
A current flows through the external circuit until the electrochemical potentials of the electrodes equalize and the driving force vanishes.
Under these conditions, the reaction proceeds spontaneously and the system operates as a \emph{galvanic cell}.

During charging, the applied current reverses the direction of the electrochemical processes: reduction occurs at the negative electrode and oxidation at the positive one. 
In this case, the system functions as an \emph{electrolytic cell}, and the reaction requires external work.

\subsection{Electrode potentials and cell voltage}

The electrochemical potential of an ionic species $i$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mu}_i = \mu_i + z_i F \phi,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential, $z_i$ the charge number, $F$ Faraday's constant and $\phi$ the electrostatic potential. 
For a redox reaction of the form
\[
\mathrm{Ox} + ne^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Red},
\]
the equilibrium potential follows the Nernst equation
\begin{equation}
    E = E^\circ + \frac{RT}{nF} \ln\!\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm{Ox}}}{a_{\mathrm{Red}}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $a$ denotes activity and $E^\circ$ the standard reduction potential. 
In a full cell, the open-circuit voltage (OCV) corresponds to the difference between the equilibrium potentials of the positive and negative electrodes:
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{OCV}} = E_+ - E_-.
\end{equation}

\section{Performance metrics}

Battery performance is commonly assessed in terms of energy, power and capacity.

The gravimetric specific energy is defined as
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{sp}} = \int U_{\mathrm{load}}(t)\, I(t)\, dt,
\end{equation}
while the specific power is given by
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{sp}} = \frac{U_{\mathrm{load}}(t) I(t)}{m_{\mathrm{cell}}}.
\end{equation}

The theoretical capacity of an intercalation material accommodating $n$ electrons per formula unit is
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{th}} = \frac{n F}{M},
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the molar mass. 
For a composite electrode with active mass $m_{\mathrm{act}}$, the corresponding theoretical specific capacity is $q_{\mathrm{th}} = Q_{\mathrm{th}} m_{\mathrm{act}}$.

In practice, side reactions, rate limitations and electrode mesostructure (porosity and thickness) reduce the accessible capacity.

In battery technology, the applied current is commonly expressed as C-rate, defined as the charge or discharge current normalized by the nominal capacity $Q_{\mathrm{nom}}$:
\begin{equation}
    I = C\, Q_{\mathrm{nom}}.
\end{equation}
A 1C current corresponds to a full charge or discharge in one hour, whereas C/10 corresponds to ten hours.

\section{Storage mechanisms}

Electrochemical storage relies on redox reactions at the battery electrodes, which may proceed through different mechanisms. 
Early batteries, such as Alessandro Volta’s pile, operated through metal electrodissolution and deposition, whereas modern rechargeable batteries primarily exploit ion intercalation.
These mechanisms are not specific to lithium-based systems and can involve other alkali or multivalent ions, depending on the electrolyte and host structure.

\subsection{Intercalation}

In intercalation mechanisms, guest ions reversibly insert into the crystal lattice of a host material without destroying its structure. 
This behavior is typical of inorganic materials composed of stacked crystal planes bound by electrostatic forces.
Charge compensation occurs through oxidation or reduction of framework elements as ions enter or leave interlayer sites.
Representative crystal structures include layered transition-metal oxides, olivines and spinels.

In layered oxides such as cobalt or manganese oxides, guest ions occupy octahedral sites between transition-metal oxide layers. 
The intercalation reaction can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{M}_x \mathrm{A}_y + (1-x)\mathrm{M}^{n+} + (1-x)ne^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{MA}_y,
\end{equation}
where M denotes the intercalated ion, A the host framework, and $x$ varies with the state of charge. 
The layered structure provides two-dimensional diffusion pathways, enabling relatively fast ion transport.

In olivine phosphates such as LiFePO$_4$, ions migrate through one-dimensional channels. 
The resulting two-phase behavior, characterized by the coexistence of lithiated and delithiated domains, produces the well-known flat voltage plateau.

\subsection{Alloying}

In alloying mechanisms, metal ions are stored through the formation of intermetallic phases rather than by lattice insertion. 
Silicon, for example, reacts with lithium according to
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Si} + \beta \mathrm{Li}^+ + \beta e^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Li}_\beta \mathrm{Si},
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ can reach values up to 4.4, yielding a theoretical specific capacity of 4200~mAh~g$^{-1}$, nearly an order of magnitude higher than that of graphite, the current commercial standard.

The large volume expansion associated with alloying (approximately 300\% for silicon) induces mechanical stress, particle fracture and rapid degradation. 
To mitigate these effects, modern high-capacity anodes typically employ silicon--graphite composites and protective carbon coatings.

\subsection{Conversion}

Conversion reactions involve the electrochemical reduction of metal oxides or fluorides to their metallic state, accompanied by anion migration. 
These materials offer very high theoretical capacities and exhibit multiple voltage plateaus associated with sequential reduction steps. 
However, large voltage hysteresis, low coulombic efficiency and rapid capacity fade limit their practical applicability, and they remain largely confined to research studies.

\subsection{Electrodeposition}

In electrodeposition mechanisms, ions are directly reduced to their metallic form without a host structure. 
Lithium metal, for instance, provides the highest specific capacity (3861~mAh~g$^{-1}$) and the lowest electrochemical potential, enabling very high energy density cells. 
Sodium and other alkali metals offer similar advantages for their respective chemistries.
However, metallic deposition often leads to dendritic growth, increased surface area and accelerated electrolyte decomposition, resulting in reduced cycle life and safety concerns.
