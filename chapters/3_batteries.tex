\chapter{Batteries}

Electrochemical energy storage systems convert chemical energy into electrical work through redox reactions occurring at electrode interfaces.
Among these systems, rechargeable (or secondary) batteries have become essential across portable electronics, stationary storage, and electric mobility. 
Lithium-ion batteries, in particular, have dominated the field due to their high operating voltage, large specific energy, and excellent cycle efficiency. 
% This chapter introduces the fundamental principles of rechargeable batteries, their active materials, performance metrics, and modern technological evolution. 
% Emphasis is placed on lithium-ion batteries because they serve as the experimental platform in this work.

Battery typically are modelled as black-box systems, defined solely by their input and output relations (current and voltage, respectivly).
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy serves as an ideal technique to monitor the internal resistance of the battery and to estimate its state of health (SOH).
Without the separation of electrodes, impedance measurements on batteries do not probe a single interface or reaction, but rather the collective response of multiple electrodes, electrolyte phases, and transport pathways embedded within a specific cell architecture.
As a consequence, the measured impedance depends not only on intrinsic electrochemical properties, but also on the state of charge, operating history, and degradation state of the battery as a system.
Hence, in contrast to classic electrochemical characterization, the impedance of battery is measured at different states and its difference is monitored, rater then solely consider its absolute value.

This chapter introduces the fundamental principles of rechargeable batteries required to interpret impedance spectra measured at the cell level, including electrode configuration, operating states, and common degradation modes.
The focus is not on a comprehensive description of battery chemistry, but on those aspects that directly influence the structure, evolution, and variability of impedance spectra during operation and aging.

\section{Basic principles of operation}

A battery consists of two electrodes separated by an electronically insulating but ionically conductive electrolyte. 
During discharge, oxidation occurs at the negative voltage electrode, releasing electrons to the external circuit and cations to the electrolyte. 
Concurrently, reduction occurs at the positive electrode, accepting electrons and intercalating the cations arriving through the electrolyte. 
A negative current flow is produced on the external circuit of the cell until the electrodes reach the same potential and no driving force is present.
The reaction is spontaneous and the system referred as a \emph{galvanic cell}. 
The reverse processes occur during charging: the current flows on the opposite direction producing the reductive Redox half-reaction at the positive electrode and the oxidation at the negative one.
Such a system si called instead \emph{electrolytic cell} and the reaction is not spontaneous but requires external work on the system.

\subsection{Electrode potentials and cell voltage}

The electrochemical potential of an ionic species $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mu}_i = \mu_i + z_i F \phi,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential, $z_i$ the charge number, $F$ Faraday's constant and $\phi$ the electrostatic potential. 
For a redox reaction of the form
\[
\mathrm{Ox} + ne^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Red},
\]
the equilibrium potential is given by the Nernst equation
\begin{equation}
    E = E^\circ + \frac{RT}{nF} \ln\!\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm{Ox}}}{a_{\mathrm{Red}}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $a$ denotes the \emph{activity} and $E^\circ$ the \emph{standard reduction potential}. 
In a full cell, the open-circuit voltage (OCV) is the difference between the equilibrium potentials of the positive and negative electrodes:
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{OCV}} = E_+ - E_-.
\end{equation}

\section{Performance metrics}

Battery performance is evaluated using energy, power and capacity.

The specific energy (gravimetric)
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{sp}} = \int U_{\mathrm{load}}(t)\, I(t)\, dt,
\end{equation}
and specific power
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{sp}} = \frac{U_{\mathrm{load}}(t) I(t)}{m_{\mathrm{cell}}},
\end{equation}
% are commonly represented in Ragone plots showing trade-offs between the two.

The theoretical capacity of an intercalation material accommodating $n$ electrons per formula unit is
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{th}} = \frac{n F}{M},
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the molar mass. For a composite electrode of active mass $m_{\mathrm{act}}$, the theoretical specific capacity is $q_{\mathrm{th}} = Q_{\mathrm{th}} m_{\mathrm{act}}$.

The practical capacity is limited by side reactions, rate capability and electrode macroscopic structure (porosity and thickness).

% It is important to note that the achievable voltage window, the rate capability, and the long-term stability associated with these performance metrics are not intrinsic constants, but emerge from the underlying electrochemical kinetics and transport properties, which depend on the specific combination of electrode materials, electrolyte composition, and operating conditions.

In battery technology the current flowing in the cell is expressed as C-rate, defined as the charge or discharge current normalized by the nominal capacity $Q_{\mathrm{nom}}$:
\begin{equation}
    I = C\, Q_{\mathrm{nom}}.
\end{equation}
A 1C current charges or discharges the battery in 1 hour, while C/10 corresponds to 10 hours.

\section{Storage mechanisms}

The type of Redox reaction at each of the battery electrodes can be of different nature.
The first "pile" of Alessandro Volta was a battery using the electrodissolution and deposition of copper and zinc respectivly, while modern rechargible batteries are base on the much more efficient process of ion intercalation.
These mechanisms are not specific to lithium-based systems and can, in principle, involve different alkali or multivalent ions depending on the electrolyte and host structure.

\subsection{Intercalation}

In intercalation, guest metallic ions reversibly insert into the crystal lattice of a host material without disrupting its structure. 
This principles applied to inorganic materials where crystals planes are stacked and bound with electrostatic forces.
When an element of the structure undergoes oxidation or reduction to balance the overall charge an ion inserts or it is removed from the interlayer volume.
Example of such material crystal structure are octahedral transition metal oxides, olivines and spinel structures.

In layered oxides such as Cobalt or Manganese oxides, host ions occupy octahedral sites between transition-metal oxide layers. 
The intercalation reaction can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{M}_x \mathrm{A}_y + (1-x)\mathrm{M}^{n+} + (1-x)ne^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{MA}_y,
\end{equation}
where M denotes the intercalated metal ion, A is the host framework, and stoichiometry $x$ varies with state of charge. 
The layered structure provides two-dimensional ion diffusion pathways, enabling rapid ion transport.

In olivine phosphates like LiFePO$_4$, ions occupy sites within one-dimensional channels running through the crystal. 
The two-phase behavior (coexistence of intercalated and deintercalated domains) arises from the crystal structure and electrostatic interactions, producing the characteristic flat voltage plateau.

\subsection{Alloying}

In the alloying mechanism metal ions are stored in the active material through formation of intermetallic phases rather than lattice insertion. 
Silicon, for instance, forms a series of phases with lithium:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Si} + \beta \mathrm{Li}^+ + \beta e^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Li}_\beta \mathrm{Si},
\end{equation}
where $\beta$ can range up to 4.4, giving a theoretical specific capacity of 4200~mAh~g$^{-1}$, nearly ten times that of graphite the state of the art of modern commercial batteries.

However, the dramatic volume expansion accompanying alloying ($\sim 300\%$ for silicon) induces mechanical stress, particle fragmentation and rapid electrode degradation. 
Modern high-capacity anodes employ silicon-graphite composites  and carbon coatings with a small percentage of silicon to mitigate damages and maintain electrical contact across cycling.

\subsection{Conversion}

Conversion reactions involve reduction of a metal oxide or fluoride to metallic form, coupled with oxide/fluoride ion migration. 
These reactions typically exhibit multiple voltage plateaus corresponding to sequential reduction steps and offer very high theoretical capacities. 
However, conversion materials suffer from large voltage hysteresis, low coulombic efficiency and rapid capacity fade due to electrolyte decomposition and material restructuring during cycling. Consequently, they remain largely in research phase and are not employed in commercial cells.

\subsection{Electrodeposition}

Ions can be also converted directly to their metallic form tand avoid any host structure through electrodeposition and dissolution of the metal itself. 
Lithium metal as negative electrode offers the highest specific capacity (3861~mAh~g$^{-1}$) and lowest potential for lithium-ion batteries, enabling very high energy density cells. 
Also sodium and other alkali metals provide similar benefits for their respective battery chemistries. 
However, metallic deposition is prone to dendritic growth, which increases surface area and accelerates electrolyte decomposition and loss of active material, reducing cycle life and raising safety concerns.

\subsection{Electrode porosity}

In intercalation materials, the rate capability is governed by solid-state diffusion of the guest ions. The diffusion equation for concentration $c(x,t)$ inside a spherical particle of radius $R$ is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D_s \left( \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2 \frac{\partial c}{\partial r}\right)\right),
\end{equation}
with $D_s$ the solid diffusion coefficient. 
A characteristic diffusion time is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_D = \frac{R^2}{D_s}.
\end{equation}
This time constant critically affects high-rate performance and appears later in impedance spectra as finite-length diffusion elements.

\section{Electrolytes}

Electrolytes must provide high ionic conductivity, wide electrochemical stability and compatibility with both electrodes.

Most Li-ion cells use organic carbonate solvents (EC, DEC, EMC) with lithium salts such as LiPF$_6$. 
Their voltage stability window extends to $\sim 4.5-5.0$ V vs Li but decompose at low potentials, forming the SEI.

The ionic conductivity
\[
\sigma = \sum_i z_i^2 F^2 u_i c_i,
\]
depends on mobility, concentration and viscosity. 
Solvent mixtures balance stability and conductivity.

Aqueous electrolytes have excellent conductivity but a thermodynamic stability window around 1.23 V. 
Highly concentrated systems (“water-in-salt”) extend this window to $\sim 3$ V by reducing water activity.


\section{Degradation mechanisms}

Battery degradation arises from a combination of chemical, mechanical and interfacial processes that progressively reduce the accessible lithium inventory, active material and ionic/electronic transport pathways. The evolution of degradation with cycling or calendar time directly determines the state of health (SOH). Although multiple mechanisms coexist, they are commonly grouped into three major families: loss of shuttling ions (in LIBs called loss lithium inventory or LLI), loss of active material (LAM) and impedance growth.

\subsection{Loss of shuttling ions}

Loss of shuttling ions is primarily caused by parasitic reactions that consume the ions. 
In organic electrolyte base batteries a major contributor is the continuous formation and repair of the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) at the negative electrode. 
Although a stable SEI is essential for suppressing further electrolyte reduction, mechanical stress, particle cracking, volume changes or local overpotential can expose uncovered surface and produce additional SEI growth. 
The amount of metallic ions irreversibly trapped in the SEI increases with cycling and causes a progressive loss in capacity.

Metal plating also consumes shuttling ions. When the negative electrode potential drops below 0~V vs the standard metal reduction potential, metallic ions can deposit on the surface. 
Plating is favored by high current density, low temperature or large concentration overpotentials. 
If the deposited metal remains electronically connected, it can be stripped in the following cycle; otherwise it may lose contact with the current collector, forming electrically isolated ("dead lithium" in LIBs) that irreversibly reduces the shuttling ion inventory.

\subsection{Loss of active material}

Mechanical degradation of electrode materials results in reduced electrochemically active surface. 
In positive electrodes such as layered NMCs, repeated Li intercalation and deintercalation induces anisotropic lattice strain, phase transitions and microcrack formation. 
Cracking leads to particle fragmentation and loss of contact with the conductive carbon network or current collector, increasing both the resistance and the inaccessible fraction of active material.

Graphite particles also undergo structural degradation under fast cycling due to expansion/contraction associated with staging transitions. Silicon-based anodes suffer even more severe mechanical degradation due to their large volumetric expansion, requiring advanced composite architectures to maintain structural integrity.

\subsection{Impedance growth}

With aging, the ohmic, charge-transfer and diffusion-related impedance components increase due to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item thickening of interphases at both electrodes (SEI and CEI),
    \item buildup of resistive reaction products,
    \item loss of electrolyte conductivity,
    \item increase of charge-transfer resistance due to surface restructuring,
    \item reduced effective diffusion coefficients due to material degradation.
\end{itemize}
Impedance growth not only reduces power capability but also induces non-uniform current distribution, accelerating further degradation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is therefore a powerful diagnostic tool for quantifying these aging phenomena.

\section{Electrochemical characterization of batteries}

Understanding battery performance and degradation requires controlled electrochemical testing. 
The most widely used methods rely on galvanostatic or potentiostatic excitation and analysis of voltage, current responses with time.

\subsection{Galvanostatic cycling}

Galvanostatic charge and discharge at fixed current $I$ is the standard method for determining capacity, coulombic efficiency and cycle life. 
The measured voltage $U(t)$ is the sum of OCV and overpotentials due to kinetic, ohmic and mass-transfer limitations:
\begin{equation}
    U(t) = U_{\mathrm{OCV}}(x) + \eta_{\mathrm{kin}} + \eta_{\mathrm{ohm}} + \eta_{\mathrm{mt}}.
\end{equation}
The extracted capacity is
\begin{equation}
    Q = \int I\, dt
\end{equation}
and the coulombic efficiency (CE) is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CE} = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{dis}}}{Q_{\mathrm{chg}}}.
\end{equation}
For practical Li-ion cells, CE must exceed 99.9~\% to achieve thousand-cycle lifetimes.

In galvanostatic cycling, the cell is kept between a lower and higher operation voltage (voltage limits) to avoid degradation of electrodes or electrolyte.

At high states of charge, the diffusional overpotentials limits the achievable capacity of a cell in galvanostatic operation by reaching the voltage limited.
Therefore, Li-ion cells are typically charged using a constant-current / constant-voltage (CC-CV) protocol: first a constant current up to an upper voltage limit, then a constant voltage stage at $V_{\max}$ until the current tapers below a threshold. 
The CC-CV method enables to fully charge a battery without undesired side reactions.

\subsection{Rate capability tests}

By cycling a cell at progressively increasing C-rates, the dependence of delivered capacity on current reveals transport limitations. 
The characteristic rate at which the capacity drops significantly corresponds to limitations in solid-state diffusion, electrolyte transport or internal resistance.

\subsection{Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)}

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy is extensively used in battery characterization for its capability of identifying and separate processes such as ohmic resistance, charge-transfer kinetics, double-layer capacitance, and diffusion, which kinetics evolves with the aging of the cell and the occurance of degradation pehnomenon.
The impedance of a cell is measured at different State of Charge (SOC) and State of Health (SOH) by interrupting the CCCV cycling protocol at fixed states and waiting for the relaxation of the interfaces.
A correct impedance measruement with classic sinusodial perturbation of decreasing frequency may take 30 minutes or more to probe the frequency response of a common lithium-ion battery (1MHz-10mHz).
This time inreases significanlty the experiment duration limiting its utilization for long-term explorations.
