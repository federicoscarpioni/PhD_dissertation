\chapter{Batteries}

Electrochemical energy storage systems convert chemical potential energy into electrical work through redox reactions occurring at spatially separated electrodes. Among these systems, rechargeable (secondary) batteries have become essential across portable electronics, stationary storage and electric mobility. Lithium-ion batteries in particular have dominated the field due to their high operating voltage, large specific energy and excellent cycle efficiency. This chapter introduces the fundamental principles of rechargeable batteries, their key materials, performance metrics and modern technological evolution. Emphasis is placed on lithium-ion batteries because they serve as the experimental platform in this work.

\section{Basic principles of operation}

A battery consists of two electrodes, a positive and a negative electrode (IUPAC convention), separated by an electronically insulating but ionically conductive electrolyte. During discharge (galvanic cell), oxidation occurs at the negative electrode, releasing electrons to the external circuit and cations to the electrolyte. Concurrently, reduction occurs at the positive electrode, accepting electrons and intercalating the cations arriving through the electrolyte. The reverse processes occur during charging (electrolytic cell).

\subsection{Electrode potentials and cell voltage}

The electrochemical potential of an ionic species $i$ is
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mu}_i = \mu_i + z_i F \phi,
\end{equation}
where $\mu_i$ is the chemical potential, $z_i$ the charge number, $F$ Faraday's constant and $\phi$ the electrostatic potential. For a redox reaction of the form
\[
\mathrm{Ox} + ne^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Red},
\]
the equilibrium potential is given by the Nernst equation
\begin{equation}
    E = E^\circ + \frac{RT}{nF} \ln\!\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm{Ox}}}{a_{\mathrm{Red}}}\right),
\end{equation}
where $a$ denotes activity and $E^\circ$ the standard reduction potential. In a full cell, the open-circuit voltage (OCV) is the difference between the equilibrium potentials of the positive and negative electrodes:
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{OCV}} = E_+ - E_-.
\end{equation}

Because each electrode hosts a composition-dependent chemical potential for the intercalated species, the OCV becomes a function of electrode stoichiometry. For an intercalation compound $M_x A$, where $A$ is the guest species (Li, Na, \ldots), the chemical potential is
\begin{equation}
    \mu_A(x) = \mu_A^\circ + RT \ln\!\left(\frac{x}{1 - x}\right) + \frac{\partial G_{\mathrm{mix}}}{\partial x},
\end{equation}
where $G_{\mathrm{mix}}$ accounts for non-ideal interactions. The OCV profile thus reflects phase transitions, entropy terms and the mixing thermodynamics within the active materials.

\subsection{Gibbs free energy and electrical work}

The electrical work delivered during discharge is related to the Gibbs free energy of reaction:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta G = -n F U_{\mathrm{OCV}}.
\end{equation}
For a battery cycled between states of charge with stoichiometries $x_1$ and $x_2$, the theoretical energy is
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{th}} = n F \int_{x_1}^{x_2} U_{\mathrm{OCV}}(x)\, dx.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Transport inside electrodes}

In intercalation materials, the rate capability is governed by solid-state diffusion of the guest ions. The diffusion equation for concentration $c(x,t)$ inside a spherical particle of radius $R$ is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial c}{\partial t} = D_s \left( \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2 \frac{\partial c}{\partial r}\right)\right),
\end{equation}
with $D_s$ the solid diffusion coefficient. A characteristic diffusion time is
\begin{equation}
    \tau_D = \frac{R^2}{D_s}.
\end{equation}
This time constant critically affects high-rate performance and appears later in impedance spectra as finite-length diffusion elements.

\section{Performance metrics}

Battery performance is evaluated using energy, power and capacity:

\subsection{Capacity}

The theoretical capacity of an intercalation material accommodating $n$ electrons per formula unit is
\begin{equation}
    Q_{\mathrm{th}} = \frac{n F}{M},
\end{equation}
where $M$ is the molar mass. For a composite electrode of active mass $m_{\mathrm{act}}$, the theoretical specific capacity is $q_{\mathrm{th}} = Q_{\mathrm{th}} m_{\mathrm{act}}$.

The practical capacity is limited by side reactions, rate capability and electrode macroscopic structure (porosity and thickness).

\subsection{Energy and power}

The specific energy (gravimetric)
\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{sp}} = \int U_{\mathrm{load}}(t)\, I(t)\, dt,
\end{equation}
and specific power
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{sp}} = \frac{U_{\mathrm{load}}(t) I(t)}{m_{\mathrm{cell}}},
\end{equation}
are commonly represented in Ragone plots showing trade-offs between the two.

\subsection{C-rate}

A C-rate is defined as the charge or discharge current normalized by the nominal capacity $Q_{\mathrm{nom}}$:
\begin{equation}
    I = C\, Q_{\mathrm{nom}}.
\end{equation}
A 1C current charges or discharges the battery in 1 hour, while C/10 corresponds to 10 hours.

\section{Electrode materials}

\subsection{Positive electrodes}

The positive electrode determines cell voltage and significantly influences energy density. Modern Li-ion cathodes fall into three main families:

\paragraph{Layered oxides (NMC, NCA).}  
These compounds have the general formula $\mathrm{Li}_{x}\mathrm{MO}_2$ (M = Ni, Mn, Co, Al). The redox couples involve transition-metal oxidation beyond +3. Their high operating voltage (up to 4.3–4.5 V vs Li) arises from large TM–O covalency. NMC622 and NMC811 are industry standards. Their advantages include high energy density and mature manufacturing; limitations include electrolyte oxidation, transition-metal dissolution and thermal instability at high SOC.

\paragraph{Olivine phosphates (LFP, LMFP).}  
LiFePO$_4$ and LiMnFePO$_4$ have a robust olivine structure with one-dimensional lithium channels, offering excellent thermal stability and long life. Their flat voltage plateau at 3.4 V results from a two-phase coexistence between lithiated and delithiated phases.

\paragraph{Spinels (LMO).}  
Spinel LiMn$_2$O$_4$ provides high power capability due to three-dimensional Li diffusion pathways but suffers from Mn dissolution and limited lifetime.

\subsection{Negative electrodes}

\paragraph{Graphite.}  
Graphite remains the dominant negative electrode material. Its electrochemical profile shows a series of plateaus corresponding to staging transitions:
\[
\mathrm{Li}_x\mathrm{C}_6,\quad x = 0 \to 1.
\]
The lowest potential ($\sim 0$ V vs Li) yields high energy density but lies close to lithium deposition, especially under high current densities or low temperatures.

\paragraph{Silicon and silicon-graphite blends.}  
Si can alloy with Li up to Li$_{15}$Si$_4$, giving enormous theoretical capacity. However, $\sim 300\%$ volume expansion induces mechanical stress and rapid SEI reformation, necessitating composite architectures.

\paragraph{Lithium metal.}  
Lithium metal has the highest specific capacity and lowest potential but is prone to dendritic deposition, limiting its use with liquid electrolytes. Solid-state electrolytes aim to stabilize its operation.

\section{Electrolytes}

Electrolytes must provide high ionic conductivity, wide electrochemical stability and compatibility with both electrodes.

\subsection{Organic electrolytes}

Most Li-ion cells use organic carbonate solvents (EC, DEC, EMC) with lithium salts such as LiPF$_6$. Their voltage stability window extends to $\sim 4.5-5.0$ V vs Li but decompose at low potentials, forming the SEI.

The ionic conductivity
\[
\sigma = \sum_i z_i^2 F^2 u_i c_i,
\]
depends on mobility, concentration and viscosity. Solvent mixtures balance stability and conductivity.

\subsection{Aqueous electrolytes}

Aqueous electrolytes have excellent conductivity but a thermodynamic stability window around 1.23 V. Highly concentrated systems (“water-in-salt”) extend this window to $\sim 3$ V by reducing water activity.

\section{Degradation mechanisms}

Battery degradation arises from a combination of chemical, mechanical and interfacial processes that progressively reduce the accessible lithium inventory, active material and ionic/electronic transport pathways. The evolution of degradation with cycling or calendar time directly determines the state of health (SOH). Although multiple mechanisms coexist, they are commonly grouped into three major families: loss of lithium inventory (LLI), loss of active material (LAM) and impedance growth.

\subsection{Loss of lithium inventory}

LLI is primarily caused by parasitic reactions that consume cyclable lithium. A major contributor is the continuous formation and repair of the solid-electrolyte interphase (SEI) at the negative electrode. Although a stable SEI is essential for suppressing further electrolyte reduction, mechanical stress, particle cracking, volume changes (especially in Si-based anodes) or local overpotential can expose fresh surface and trigger additional SEI growth. The amount of lithium irreversibly trapped in the SEI increases with cycling and causes a progressive loss in capacity.

Lithium plating also consumes lithium. When the anode potential drops below 0~V vs Li/Li$^+$, metallic lithium can deposit on the surface. Plating is favored by high current density, low temperature or large concentration overpotentials. If the deposited lithium remains electronically connected, it can be stripped in the following cycle; otherwise it may lose contact with the current collector, forming electrically isolated "dead lithium" that irreversibly reduces the active lithium inventory.

\subsection{Loss of active material}

Mechanical degradation of electrode materials results in reduced electrochemically active surface. In positive electrodes such as layered NMCs, repeated Li intercalation and deintercalation induces anisotropic lattice strain, phase transitions and microcrack formation. Cracking leads to particle fragmentation and loss of contact with the conductive carbon network or current collector, increasing both the resistance and the inaccessible fraction of active material.

Graphite particles also undergo structural degradation under fast cycling due to expansion/contraction associated with staging transitions. Silicon-based anodes suffer even more severe mechanical degradation due to their large volumetric expansion, requiring advanced composite architectures to maintain structural integrity.

\subsection{Impedance growth}

With aging, the ohmic, charge-transfer and diffusion-related impedance components increase due to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item thickening of interphases at both electrodes (SEI and CEI),
    \item buildup of resistive reaction products,
    \item loss of electrolyte conductivity,
    \item increase of charge-transfer resistance due to surface restructuring,
    \item reduced effective diffusion coefficients due to material degradation.
\end{itemize}
Impedance growth not only reduces power capability but also induces non-uniform current distribution, accelerating further degradation. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is therefore a powerful diagnostic tool for quantifying these aging phenomena, and will be treated in detail in Chapter~X.

\section{Electrochemical characterization of batteries}

Understanding battery performance and degradation requires controlled electrochemical testing. The most widely used methods rely on galvanostatic or potentiostatic excitation and analysis of voltage, current and time responses.

\subsection{Galvanostatic cycling}

Galvanostatic charge and discharge at fixed current $I$ is the standard method for determining capacity, coulombic efficiency and cycle life. The measured voltage response $U(t)$ includes OCV contributions and overpotentials due to kinetic, ohmic and mass-transfer limitations:
\begin{equation}
    U(t) = U_{\mathrm{OCV}}(x) + \eta_{\mathrm{kin}} + \eta_{\mathrm{ohm}} + \eta_{\mathrm{mt}}.
\end{equation}
The extracted capacity is
\begin{equation}
    Q = \int I\, dt.
\end{equation}
The coulombic efficiency (CE) is
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{CE} = \frac{Q_{\mathrm{dis}}}{Q_{\mathrm{chg}}}.
\end{equation}
For practical Li-ion cells, CE must exceed 99.9~\% to achieve thousand-cycle lifetimes.

\subsection{CC-CV charging protocol}

At high states of charge, the overpotentials limit the achievable capacity in pure galvanostatic operation. Therefore, Li-ion cells are typically charged using a constant-current / constant-voltage (CC-CV) protocol: first a constant current up to an upper voltage limit $U_{\max}$, then a constant voltage stage at $U_{\max}$ until the current tapers below a threshold. The CC-CV method enables fuller lithiation without exceeding voltage or plating limits.

\subsection{Rate capability tests}

By cycling a cell at progressively increasing C-rates, the dependence of delivered capacity on current reveals transport limitations. The characteristic rate at which the capacity drops significantly corresponds to limitations in solid-state diffusion, electrolyte transport or internal resistance.

\subsection{Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS)}

EIS characterizes the response of the cell to small-signal perturbations in frequency domain. The complex impedance $Z(\omega)$ embeds contributions from ohmic resistance, charge-transfer kinetics, double-layer capacitance, interphase layers and diffusion. The Warburg impedance,
\begin{equation}
    Z_W(\omega) = \sigma_W (1 - j)\, \omega^{-1/2},
\end{equation}
appears for semi-infinite diffusion, while finite-length diffusion yields a transmission-line-type behavior. EIS is central to this thesis and will be discussed extensively in Chapter~X.

\section{Fast charging}

Fast charging is desirable in electric mobility but presents severe constraints for battery safety and longevity. Charging at high C-rate leads to large concentration gradients through the electrode thickness and increases the anode overpotential. If the anode potential approaches 0~V vs Li/Li$^+$, lithium plating becomes thermodynamically possible. The diffusion-limited overpotential during high-rate lithiation can be approximated by
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathrm{diff}} \approx \frac{RT}{F} \left( \frac{I}{I_{\mathrm{lim}} - I} \right),
\end{equation}
where $I_{\mathrm{lim}}$ is the limiting current associated with mass transport. Because $I_{\mathrm{lim}} \propto D_s$ and $D_s$ decreases at low temperature or in highly lithiated graphite, the risk of plating increases in cold or high-SOC conditions. Consequently, practical fast charging is performed in the SOC window 20–80~\%, often with sophisticated dynamic current profiles optimized to avoid plating.

Materials research for fast charging focuses on graphite with high-rate morphology, silicon-graphite blends, transition-metal oxides with optimized particle architecture, and advanced electrolytes/additives to stabilize the SEI.

\section{Nomenclature}

Positive and negative electrodes are the IUPAC-recommended terms for battery operation regardless of current direction. In spontaneous discharge (Galvanic cell), the positive electrode is the cathode and undergoes reduction, while the negative electrode acts as the anode. During charging (electrolytic cell), the current direction reverses, but the electrode names positive and negative are retained to avoid confusion. Though, in the battery literature and specific language, scientist tend to use the term cathod and anode to indicate the postive and negative electrode. In an electrolytic cell, the role of anode and cathod are reversed. Probably this convention is of historical reason from the material science of primary batteries that are not recharged.

The common term \textit{capacity} strictly refers to the total amount of charge, measured in Ah in teh battery community or Coulombs according to the International System of Measures. It must not be confused with capacitance of a capacitor electrical elemetn. Throughout this thesis, the IUPAC convention will be followed.

\section{Battery management systems (BMS)}

The battery management system (BMS) is the supervisory electronic unit responsible for monitoring, controlling and protecting a battery pack during operation. A battery pack is a connection of batteries in series or parallel combined to obtain a certain power output. The BMS plays a critical role in ensuring safety, maximizing performance, extending lifetime and providing accurate estimates of internal states that cannot be measured directly.

\subsection{Core functions of a BMS}

The principal functions of a BMS include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Voltage measurement and protection:} maintaining all cells within their safe operating voltage window.
    \item \textbf{Current monitoring and control:} enforcing charge/discharge current limits to avoid thermal runaway or lithium plating.
    \item \textbf{Temperature monitoring:} collecting data from multiple sensors and controlling the thermal management system.
    \item \textbf{State estimation:} estimating state of charge (SOC), state of health (SOH), state of power (SOP) and internal resistance.
    \item \textbf{Cell balancing:} equalizing charge among cells to avoid imbalance and premature degradation.
    \item \textbf{Communication:} interfacing with vehicle control units or stationary system controllers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{State estimation}

SOC estimation typically combines coulomb counting, OCV lookup tables and equivalent-circuit models. SOH estimation often relies on capacity fade tracking or increases in resistance. However, these traditional methods face challenges under dynamic load profiles or aging-related drift.

Model-based estimation methods integrate electrochemical models or pseudo-two-dimensional (P2D) models to increase accuracy. Kalman filters, particle filters and machine-learning methods are widely used in modern BMS implementations.

\subsection{Impedance-based diagnostics}

Electrochemical impedance contains rich information on reaction kinetics, SEI resistance, charge-transfer processes, concentration gradients and diffusion limitations. Therefore, impedance is a powerful tool for BMS diagnostics.

However, conventional frequency-sweep EIS is too slow for embedded systems. Modern research explores:
\begin{itemize}
    \item on-board fast EIS using multisine or perturb-and-observe techniques,
    \item online impedance tracking during dynamic driving cycles,
    \item data-driven impedance models to estimate SOC and SOH.
\end{itemize}

This thesis contributes to this direction by developing instrumentation for continuous impedance measurement using multisine excitation under galvanostatic cycling. The goal is to extract time-varying parametric transfer functions for predictive SOH estimation. The long-term perspective is to integrate such impedance-based diagnostics into a ``smart BMS'' capable of adaptive control and health prediction at cell level.
