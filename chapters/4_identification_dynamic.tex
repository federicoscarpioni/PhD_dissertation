\chapter{Identification of dynamical systems}
\label{ch:system_identification}

Electrochemical systems such as batteries are dynamical systems in the sense that their behavior is governed by differential equations describing the time evolution of internal variables, including ionic concentrations, electrode potentials and temperature, under given operating conditions.

Electrochemical impedance is commonly interpreted as a frequency-dependent material or interfacial property, yet its definition originates from linear system theory.
Impedance corresponds to the frequency response of a system obtained by linearizing its input-output behavior around a given operating point, under assumptions of linearity, causality, and time invariance.

These assumptions determine whether an impedance spectrum is physically valid.
When they are violated, as in the presence of drift, nonlinearity, or strong operating transients, the measured frequency response function may no longer correspond to a true impedance, but to an approximation whose validity must be carefully assessed.

This chapter introduces the elements of system theory required to formalize the concept of impedance, including linear time-invariant systems, transfer functions, and frequency-domain representations.
The aim is not to develop system theory in full generality, but to establish a precise framework within which electrochemical impedance measurements can be defined, and later extended to non-stationary conditions.

\section{Dynamical systems}

A dynamical system is defined as a system whose evolution in time is governed by a set of differential equations.
In general form, the dynamics can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d\mathbf{x}(t)}{dt} = \mathbf{f}\!\left(\mathbf{x}(t), \mathbf{u}(t), t\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}(t)$ is the vector of state variables and $\mathbf{u}(t)$ represents external inputs.

In electrochemical systems, the state variables include quantities such as ionic concentrations, electric potentials, temperature and interfacial coverages.
The governing equations typically consist of coupled partial differential equations arising from mass transport, charge conservation and reaction kinetics.

\section{System identification}

System identification is the discipline concerned with building mathematical models of dynamical systems based on measured input--output data.
Rather than deriving models exclusively from first principles, system identification seeks to infer system behavior experimentally by applying controlled excitations and analyzing the resulting responses.

The identified model does not necessarily coincide with the full physical description of the system.
Instead, it represents an effective input--output relationship that is valid within a specified operating regime and under given assumptions.
In this sense, impedance spectroscopy constitutes a system identification experiment performed in the frequency domain.

\section{Linearization and time-invariant approximation}

Electrochemical systems are inherently nonlinear and time-varying.
However, system identification commonly relies on linear approximations around a given operating point.

Let $\mathbf{x}_0$ denote a nominal state.
For sufficiently small perturbations, the system dynamics can be linearized as
\begin{equation}
    \delta \left( \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t} \right) = \mathbf{A}\,\delta\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\,\delta\mathbf{u}(t),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are Jacobian matrices evaluated at the operating point.

If the operating point evolves slowly compared to the timescale of the excitation, the system can be treated as locally time-invariant.
This assumption underlies the interpretation of impedance as a linear frequency response.

A system is said to be \emph{linear} if it satisfies the superposition principle, meaning that the response to a linear combination of inputs equals the corresponding linear combination of the individual responses.
Formally, for any inputs $\mathbf{u}_1(t)$ and $\mathbf{u}_2(t)$ and scalars $\alpha$, $\beta$, linearity implies
\[
\mathbf{y}\!\left(\alpha \mathbf{u}_1 + \beta \mathbf{u}_2\right)
= \alpha \mathbf{y}\!\left(\mathbf{u}_1\right) + \beta \mathbf{y}\!\left(\mathbf{u}_2\right).
\]

A system is \emph{time-invariant} if its input--output relationship does not depend explicitly on absolute time.
If an input $\mathbf{u}(t)$ produces an output $\mathbf{y}(t)$, then a time-shifted input $\mathbf{u}(t - t_0)$ produces the correspondingly shifted output $\mathbf{y}(t - t_0)$ for any $t_0$.

When both properties hold, the system is referred to as a \emph{linear time-invariant} (LTI) system.
Under the assumptions of small-signal linearization and sufficiently slow evolution of the operating point, electrochemical systems can be locally approximated as LTI over finite time intervals.
% This approximation underpins the definition of impedance as a transfer function and is invoked throughout the remainder of this thesis.

\section{Time-domain representation}

In linear time-invariant form, a dynamical system can be represented in the so called "state-space model" as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}(t)}{\mathrm{d}t} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}(t),
    \label{eq:state sapce input}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{y}(t) = \mathbf{C}\mathbf{x}(t) + \mathbf{D}\mathbf{u}(t),
    \label{eq:state sapce output}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{y}(t)$ denotes the measured output.

In electrochemistry, time-domain identification methods are commonly employed to estimate transport parameters from transient experiments.
A notable example is the galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT), in which diffusion coefficients are estimated from the relaxation of the electrode potential following a current step.
However, electrochemical systems exhibit a wide spectrum of characteristic timescales.
Charge-transfer reactions, adsorption, and interfacial transport phenomena occur at timescales several orders of magnitude shorter than bulk diffusion.
Capturing the full system dynamics in the time domain would therefore require both very high temporal resolution to resolve fast processes and very long experiments to observe slow relaxation, which is often impractical.
Moreover, the superposition of multiple coupled processes leads to transient responses that are difficult to model using simple exponential or power law functions.
While diffusion-dominated behavior can be interpreted in the time domain under restrictive assumptions, the simultaneous presence of faster interfacial phenomena complicates parameter identification.

Frequency-domain identification offers a better solution for addressing this complexity separating processes according to their characteristic time constant.

\section{Frequency-domain identification}

\subsection{From differential equations to frequency domain}
\label{sec:laplace}

Linearized electrochemical models are commonly expressed as ordinary or partial differential equations in time.
The Laplace transform was introduced as a method to convert linear differential equations into algebraic equations in a complex variable, exploiting the well known mathematical framework of linear algebra to solve them.
In doing so, it replaces differentiation with multiplication by the complex frequency variable \( s \).

For a time-dependent function \( x(t) \), defined for \( t \geq 0 \), the Laplace transform is defined as
\begin{equation}
X(s) = \mathcal{L}\{x(t)\} = \int_{0}^{\infty} x(t)\,e^{-s t}\,\mathrm{d}t,
\end{equation}
where \( s = \sigma + j\omega \) is a complex variable.

A key property of the Laplace transform is that differentiation in time corresponds to multiplication in the Laplace domain:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}\right\} = s X(s) - x(0).
\end{equation}
Under zero initial conditions, which are typically assumed in impedance analysis, this reduces to a simple algebraic relation.

Furthermore, when the real part \( \sigma \) of the complex variable \( s = \sigma + j\omega \) is set to zero, the Laplace transform formally reduces to the Fourier transform, provided that the integral converges.
This condition is satisfied for linear time-invariant and stable systems subjected to bounded excitations, as typically assumed in impedance spectroscopy.

Under these assumptions, the system dynamics can be characterized by evaluating the transfer function along the imaginary axis \( s = j\omega \).
This evaluation corresponds to the steady-state response to sinusoidal perturbations and motivates the term \emph{frequency-domain} characterization.

\subsection{Fourier Transform}

The Fourier transform provides a mathematical framework to represent time-dependent signals as a superposition of sinusoidal components.
Given a continuous-time signal \( x(t) \), its Fourier transform \( X(\omega) \) is defined as
\begin{equation}
X(\omega) =
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
x(t)\, e^{-j\omega t}\, dt,
\end{equation}
while the inverse Fourier transform reconstructs the original signal:
\begin{equation}
x(t) =
\frac{1}{2\pi}
\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
X(\omega)\, e^{j\omega t}\, d\omega.
\end{equation}

In the context of system identification, the Fourier transform enables the analysis of linear systems in the frequency domain by converting differential equations in time into algebraic relations in frequency.
For a linear time-invariant system, the response to a sinusoidal excitation at angular frequency \( \omega \) is also sinusoidal at the same frequency, differing only in amplitude and phase.
This property allows the system behavior to be fully characterized by its frequency response.

\subsection{Definition of the transfer function}
\label{sec:transfer function def diff equations}
Consider a linearized dynamical system described by a differential equation of the form
\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}y(t)}{\mathrm{d}t} + a\,y(t) = b\,u(t),
\end{equation}
where \( u(t) \) is an input perturbation and \( y(t) \) is the measured response.

Applying the Laplace transform under zero initial conditions yields
\begin{equation}
s Y(s) + a Y(s) = b U(s),
\end{equation}
which can be rearranged to give
\begin{equation}
Y(s) = \frac{b}{s + a} U(s).
\end{equation}

The transfer function is defined as the ratio between the Fourier-transformed output and input:
\begin{equation}
G(s) = \frac{Y(s)}{U(s)}
\label{eq:transfer function dy sys}
\end{equation}
and it describes how the system responds with the output \( Y(s) \) to certain input \( U(s) \).
Equation \ref{eq:transfer function dy sys} resembles the definition of impedance reported in chapter \ref{ch:electrochemistry} that is, in fact, the same mathematical object for electrochemical systems.

\subsection{Connection to state-space representations}

The same transfer function can be derived from a state-space model defined from equations \cite{eq:state sapce input} and \cite{eq:state sapce output} applying the Laplace transform, which yields to the expression 
\begin{equation}
G(s) = \textbf{C} (s\textbf{I} - \textbf{A})^{-1} \textbf{B} + \textbf{D},
\end{equation}
where $\textbf{I}$ is the identify matrix.
This expression demonstrates that state-space and transfer-function models are mathematically equivalent descriptions of the same linear system.
The choice between time or frequency domain characterization depends on the intended application.

\section{Experimental estimation of frequency response functions}

The transfer function is a mathematical concept deduced from a first-principles model of a system subjected to a small periodic perturbation of frequency \( f = \omega/2 \pi \).
In practical settings, a system at steady state can be perturbed with a small sinusoidal excitation to establish the linear and time-invariant conditions and estimate the \emph{frequency response function} (FRF) at frequencies \( f_k \).
In other words, the frequency response function is the practical realization of the transfer function.

While the transfer function is a theoretical object and LTI by definition, an experimental frequency response function is an approximation of it. 
The validity of the approximation depends on the condition in which the experiment is performed.
The Kramers--Kronig relations as a test for validity of the transfer functions were discussed in section \ref{sec:kk} (p. \pageref{sec:kk}).

\section{Parameter estimation from impedance spectra}

Once a frequency response function has been measured for a given system and validated, the goal of system identification is to infer a mathematical model that represents the underlying transfer function.
In this work, the focus is placed on parametric models, which are defined by a finite set of parameters.
Such models may be derived from first-principles physical assumptions or formulated as more general mathematical expressions, such as rational functions or polynomials.
In both cases, the resulting models are typically nonlinear with respect to their parameters.

\subsection{Fitting procedure}
\label{sec:fitting-sys-id}
After selecting a model structure, parameter estimation is performed by fitting the model to the experimentally measured complex-valued impedance data.
The fitting procedure consists of solving an optimization problem to determine the parameter vector \( \boldsymbol{\theta} \) that minimizes the discrepancy between the model response \( Z_{\text{model}}(\omega_k; \boldsymbol{\theta}) \) and the experimental data \( Z_{\text{exp}}(\omega_k) \), up to a measurement error \( \epsilon_Z \).
This relationship can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
Z_{\text{exp}}(\omega_k) =
Z_{\text{model}}(\omega_k; \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \epsilon_Z.
\end{equation}

The optimization problem is commonly formulated as a least-squares minimization \cite{pintelonTransferFunctions1994,orazemEIS}.
Since the impedance is a complex quantity, the real and imaginary parts are treated jointly in the definition of the objective function:
\begin{equation}
J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) =
\sum_{k=1}^{N}
w_k
\left\|
Z_{\text{exp}}(\omega_k) -
Z_{\text{model}}(\omega_k; \boldsymbol{\theta})
\right\|^2,
\label{eq:nlls_basic}
\end{equation}
where \( w_k \) is a weighting factor associated with the measurement uncertainty at angular frequency \( \omega_k \).
This approach is commonly referred to as complex nonlinear least squares (CNLS).

In practice, the measurement uncertainty is not uniform across the frequency range.
At low frequencies, impedance measurements are generally affected by larger noise and drift, often proportional to  \(1/|Z|\).
Appropriate weighting of the residuals is therefore essential to avoid overemphasizing low-frequency data and to obtain physically meaningful parameter estimates.

The objective function is evaluated iteratively for different parameter values in order to locate its minimum.
Most impedance fitting routines rely on derivative-based optimization algorithms, which exploit local gradient information in the parameter space.
These methods require the computation of the Jacobian matrix of the residuals with respect to the parameters, either analytically or by numerical differentiation.

\subsection{Initial parameters and local minima}

A major challenge in impedance model fitting arises from the fact that the objective function \( J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) \) is non-convex in non-linear problems.
The associated hypersurface in parameter space may exhibit multiple local minima, strong parameter correlations and flat directions, and narrow valleys and sharp ridges.

As a consequence, gradient-based optimization algorithms converge to a local minimum that depends on the initial parameter guess.
There is no guarantee that this minimum corresponds to the global optimum or to a physically meaningful parameter set.

This issue becomes more pronounced for models with a large number of parameters or when fitting broadband impedance spectra spanning several decades of frequency.

The choice of initial parameters therefore plays a critical role in the success of nonlinear optimization.
Well-informed initial guesses can significantly improve convergence speed and increase the likelihood of identifying the global minimum.

In principle, global optimization strategies based on random or systematic sampling of the parameter space can be employed, followed by local refinement of the best candidates.
While such approaches improve robustness, they are often computationally expensive and may become impractical for complex models or large datasets.
