Classical electrochemical impedance spectroscopy relies on the assumption that the system under investigation is linear and time invariant (LTI) over the duration of the measurement.
Under these conditions, frequency-domain techniques introduced in the previous chapter allow the impedance to be estimated reliably from periodic perturbations.
In many electrochemical systems of practical interest, however, this assumption does not hold.
Processes such as charging and discharging of batteries, surface modification, aging, or phase transformations introduce a slow but continuous evolution of the system state during the measurement.
When the characteristic time scale of this evolution becomes comparable to the duration required to probe low frequencies, the interpretation of impedance spectra obtained with conventional methods becomes ambiguous.

This chapter reviews the methodological developments that extend impedance spectroscopy beyond the stationary regime.
Emphasis is placed on multi-frequency excitation, time-frequency analysis, and time-resolved impedance estimation, highlighting the assumptions, limitations, and validity domains of each approach.
These methods form the conceptual basis for the experimental implementation described in Chapter \ref{ch:acquisition}.

\section{Reducing acquisition time}

The challenge of measuring impedance in non-stationary electrochemical systems has been recognized since the early development of EIS.
Over the decades, several conceptual strategies have been proposed to overcome the stationarity constraint.
All the method share a common principle: reducing the time of the acquisition of alle frequencies such that the system can be considered "temporarly" time-invariant.
One of the earliest and most widely adopted strategies to address non-stationarity is the reduction of measurement time through broadband excitation that contain multiple frequencies simultanously instead of a single sinusoidal excitation.
The most used broadband excitation is the multisine, a sum of sinudoidal waves with different frequencies, beacuse it gives the better signal-to-noise ratio \cite{creasonFourierTransformFaradaic1973a}.

In classical single-sine EIS, the impedance spectrum is obtained by sequentially exciting the system at individual frequencies. The time require to collect one spectra is 
\begin{equation}
    T = n \sum_k \frac{1}{f_k},
\end{equation}
where $f_k$ are the exciteted frequencies and $n$ is the number of repetition to avarge on for computing the impedance.
On the other hand, in an experiment with multisine the time required is
\begin{equation}
    T = \frac{1}{f_{min}},
\end{equation}
where $f_min$ is the minimum excited frequency.
Depending on the choice of $n$ and the freuquency grid, the time required for the stepped method is 3 to 10 times longer \cite{schoukensBroadbandSteppedSine1999}.
Figure \ref{fig:multisine length} reports the comparison of duration of single sine sweeps with different repetions $n$ and a single multisine period; the frequency range is 10mHZ-1kHz logarithmically distributed with 8 point per decade.

The impedance at each angular frequency $\omega_k$ is then obtained by isolating the corresponding spectral components of the voltage and current signals through Fourier analysis:
\begin{equation}
Z(\omega_k) = \frac{\mathcal{F}\{v(t)\}(\omega_k)}{\mathcal{F}\{i(t)\}(\omega_k)}.
\end{equation}

The use of multisine excitation was explored as early as the 1970s, notably by Kojima and Fujiwara \cite{kojima1971fourier}, Glover and Smith \cite{gloverAlternatingCurrentPolarography1973}, and Creason and Smith \cite{creasonFourierTransformFaradaic1972}, who demonstrated its advantages in terms of measurement speed and signal-to-noise ratio.
While initially developed for stationary systems, multisine methods naturally lend themselves to applications involving slow system drift.

\subsection{Short-time Fourier transform impedance}

When a non-stationary system drifts slower than the period of the applyed multisine, the stationarity assumption is valid over the time window equal to the inverse of the period.
An "instantanous" impedance can be defined as the ration between the Fourier transform (denoted $\mathcal{F}$) of the voltage $v(t)$ and current $i(t)$ signals:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stft}
    Z(\omega, \tau) = \frac{\mathcal{F}[v(t) w(t-\tau)](\omega)}{\mathcal{F}[i(t)w(t-\tau)](\omega)},    
\end{equation}
where $w(t)$ is the window function and $\tau$ is the the time resolution of the impedance, equivalent to the window length.
The STFT method is graphically summarized in Figure \ref{fig: stft scheme}
This approach was introduced by Bond, Schwall and Smith in 1977 \cite{schwallOnlineFastFourier1977} used a microcontroller-based online fourier analysis during cyclic voltammetry experiments.

\end{figure}

\section{The limiting lower frequency}

The STFT method is based on the idea that the system is temporary stationary for the time of the window length, but how to determine the lowest frequency that respects the LTI condition?
In the literature have been proposed a couple of formulas that applies to cyclic voltammetry multisine experiments on redox couples\cite{sacciDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2009}\cite{waltersWeakAdsorptionAnions2001}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{F\nu}{RT} \ll 2\pi f_{min}
\end{equation}
based on the scan rate $\nu$ of the voltage input and 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}i}{\text{d}t} \ll 2\pi A_{min} f_{min}
\end{equation}
based on the derivative of the ouput currnt $i$, and $A_{min}$ is the amplitude of the system output at frequency $f_{min}$.
These formulas present a general inequality and it is not possible to define how greter the minimum frequency should be compared to the speed of the drift. 
A few time might be sufficient or maybe one or more orders of magnitude is needed.
Furthermore they don't generalize to other electrochemical systems or other other drift types outside cyclic voltammetry.

The only solution is to proceed by tentative acquiring the impedance at a low desired frequency and test the linearity and stationarity condition via a Kramer-Kroning test.

\section{Dynamic Multi-Frequency Analysis}
\label{sec:dmfa description}

Even when the lowest frequency of the multisine is fast enough to estimate a correctr frequency respone function, the STFT method has a limitation due to the reprocity of time and frequency.
In fact, if the window is short, the frequency resolution in the Fourir secptrum is poor (see Figure \ref{fig:frequency resolution periods}) and it is not possible to identify the lower frequency band.
This problem is worsened by the presence of a strong pink noise in this region as well as the zero-frquency skirt produced by the drift.

    \caption{Increased resolution (red ticks) when more periods of a wave are sampled.}
    \label{fig:frequency resolution periods}

To increase the frequency resolution La Mantia, Battistel, and Koster introduced the \emph{dynamic multi-frequency analysis} (DMFA) as a method to estimate instantaneous impedance spectra during fast electrochemical experiments while retaining access to low-frequency information \cite{kosterDynamicImpedanceSpectroscopy2017, battistelPhysicalDefinitionDynamic2019}.

The key idea of DMFA is to record the entire multi-frequency signals of the experiment and perform a \emph{frequency-selective analysis} of the measured signals.
Rather than windowing the signal in time, the DMFA isolates individual frequency components in the frequency domain using narrow band-pass filters and reconstructs their time evolution via inverse Fourier transformation.
The time resolution of the instantanous impedance is not anymore determined by the window length, but by the bandwidth of the band-pass filter.

Let $V(\omega)$ and $I(\omega)$ denote the Fourier transforms of the measured voltage and current signals obtained during a dynamic experiment with a multisine excitation.
For a given excitation frequency $\omega_k$, DMFA estimates the instantaneous impedance as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:formual DMFA}
Z(\omega_k, \tau) =
\frac{
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[ V(\omega)\, F(\omega - \omega_k) \right](\tau)
}{
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[ I(\omega)\, F(\omega - \omega_k) \right](\tau)
},
\end{equation}
where $F(\omega - \omega_k)$ is a flat top filter function centered at $\omega_k$, $\mathcal{F}^{-1}$ denotes the inverse Fourier transform, and $\tau$ represents time.
This operation extracts the contribution of a single excitation frequency and reconstructs its time-dependent amplitude and phase.
The steps of a DMFA algorithm are depicted schematically in Figure \ref{fig:dmfa steps}. In Step 3 one notice that the peacks corresponding on the freuquency component are not sharp but broadened; that is the effect of the time-invariance.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width =\textwidth]{figures/deis_state_art/DMFA steps.png}
    \caption{Visualization of the step in the algorithm to estimate impedance via DMFA. The steps are repeated for each frequency component to produce the entire frequencu spectrum. Adapted from \cite{piantaEvaluationKineticParameters2023a}.}
    \label{fig:dmfa steps}
\end{figure}

As filter function the author proposed a symmetric Fermi-Diract function \cite{battistel2019physical}, defined as 
\begin{equation}
g(\omega - \omega_k) =
\frac{[1 + \exp(-n)]^2}
{[1 + \exp(-n(\omega - \omega_k + bw)/bw)]
 [1 + \exp(n(\omega - \omega_k - bw)/bw)]},
\end{equation}
where the parameter $n$ controls the steepness of the filter edges and $bw$ is the bandwidth and its minimum value correspond to the maximum time resolution $\tau$.
The filter shape, reported in Figure \ref{fig:fermi dirac filter} resembles a rounded rectangle in the frequency domain, ensuring smooth transitions and minimizing spectral leakage.

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{figures/deis_state_art/symmetric fermi dirac.jpg}
    \caption{A symmetric Fermi-Dirac function used as bass badn filter, a) in the frequency domain, b) in the time domain. Differnt values of $n$ produce different the roll-offs. Adapted from \cite{battistelPhysicalDefinitionDynamic2019}}.
    \label{fig:fermi dirac filter}
\end{figure}

The choice of the bandwidth $bw$ and attenuation parameter $n$ play a big role in the quality of the estimated impedance.
In fact, they shape the width and lateral oscillatory behaviour of the equivalent time-domain window \cite{chukwuStatisticalAnalysisMeasurement2022}.
An optimal combinatio of $bw$ and $n$ ensures that two consecutive impedance are indipendent from the information of the other.

When using this type of filter with the DMFA, the resulting impedance dataset soffers of high noise at the extremes beacuse the filter function brings the signal values to zero before the inverse transform is performed.
As a consequnce some of the first and last spectrum of a dataset are always discarded.
Other filter shall be investigated to mitigate this problem.

\subsection{Relation to STFT and interpretation}

From a signal-processing perspective, DMFA can be interpreted as a generalization of the STFT.
When the filter bandwidth is increased to include the full excitation spectrum, the DMFA formulation analytically converges to the STFT-based impedance estimation.
Conversely, narrow filters allow individual frequency components to be tracked independently over time.

In the time domain, the filtering operation corresponds to a convolution of the original signal with the impulse response of the filter, resulting in a smoothed, frequency-specific representation.
This behavior is conceptually related to wavelet transforms, although DMFA is derived directly from Fourier-domain considerations and tailored to multisine excitation.

\section{Best linear time-varying approximation}

The STFT and DMFA methods exploits the temporary stationarity of a system applying a signal processing algorithm.
A different appraoch was presented in 2012 by Bruegelmans, Latier and their colleagues recontructing the best linear time-varying approximation of the outut signal of a non-stationary system exposed to multisine perturbation \cite{breugelmansOddRandomPhase2012}.

The principle is that a linear time-varying system can be espressed as a sum of $N_p$ parallel connected LTI systems $G_p$ muiltiplied by time-varying gains $b_p(t)$ such that the relation between an input $u(t)$ and output $y(t)$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = \sum _{p=0}^{N_p} G_p{u(t)}b_p(t).
\end{equation}

In practice, the BLTVA method consist in performing a parametric fitting of the output Fourer spectrum to approximate the linear and time-invariante response. 
Noise, non-linearities \cite{hallemansNonparametricIdentificationLinear2020, hallemansDetectionClassificationQuantification2021}, and non-stationarities \cite{hallemansBestLinearTimeVarying2021} are estimated form the residues of the fitting.

After the fitting the instantantneous imepdance is estimated as alwasy as ration of input and output:
\begin{align}\label{eq:bltva_impedance}
    Z_{inst} (\omega_k, \tau) =  \frac{U(\omega_k)}{Y(\omega_k, \tau)} = \frac{U(\omega_k)}{\sum_{p=0}^{N_p} \theta_{p,k}\, b_p(\tau)}, 
\end{align}
where $U$ and $Y$ are Fourier transforms of $u$ and $y$, respectively, and $\theta_{p,k}$ are optimized parameters. 

The group applied the method for the identification of the transfer function of many highly non-stationary electrochemical systems under constant current drift: metal electrodeposition \cite{colletOperandoORPEISStudy2021} \cite{dabirihavighOperandoOddRandom2022}, the conversion of zinc to zirconia \cite{dabirihavighOperandoOddRandom2023}, to study fast charging \cite{hallemansOperandoElectrochemicalImpedance2022} and SEI formation in Li-ion batteries \cite{dabirihavighOperandoORPEISMonitoring2025}.

\subsection{Comparison with DMFA}
The BLTVA estimation is simalr to the DMFA in the sense that the voltage and current signals are Fourier transformed in its entirety before the fitting.
Furthermore the fitting is performed in the frequency band ($\{\omega_{k-1}, \omega_k, \omega_{k+1}\}$) which is equivalent to a rectangular band-pass filter.

The advantage of the BLTVA is that the resulting impedance is LTI by definition and doens't require a validation, but its excecution is computationally more expensive because it requires an iterative regression procedure.
