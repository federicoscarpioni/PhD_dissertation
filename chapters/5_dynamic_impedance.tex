Classical electrochemical impedance spectroscopy relies on the assumption that the system under investigation is linear and time invariant (LTI) over the duration of the measurement.
Under these conditions, frequency-domain techniques introduced in the previous chapter allow the impedance to be estimated reliably from periodic perturbations.
In many electrochemical systems of practical interest, however, this assumption does not hold.
Processes such as charging and discharging of batteries, surface modification, aging, or phase transformations introduce a slow but continuous evolution of the system state during the measurement.
When the characteristic time scale of this evolution becomes comparable to the duration required to probe low frequencies, the interpretation of impedance spectra obtained with conventional methods becomes ambiguous.

This chapter reviews the methodological developments that extend impedance spectroscopy beyond the stationary regime.
Emphasis is placed on multi-frequency excitation, time-frequency analysis, and time-resolved impedance estimation, highlighting the assumptions, limitations, and validity domains of each approach.
These methods form the conceptual basis for the experimental implementation described in Chapter \ref{ch:acquisition}.

\section{Reducing acquisition time}

The challenge of measuring impedance in non-stationary electrochemical systems has been recognized since the early development of EIS.
Over the decades, several conceptual strategies have been proposed to overcome the stationarity constraint.
All methods share a common principle: reducing the acquisition time for all frequencies so that the system can be considered "temporarily" time-invariant.
One of the earliest and most widely adopted strategies to address non-stationarity is reducing measurement time through broadband excitation containing multiple frequencies simultaneously, rather than a single sinusoidal excitation.
The most widely used broadband excitation is the multisine—a sum of sinusoidal waves with different frequencies—because it yields the best signal-to-noise ratio \cite{creasonFourierTransformFaradaic1973a}.

In classical single-sine EIS, the impedance spectrum is obtained by sequentially exciting the system at individual frequencies. The time required to collect one spectrum is
\begin{equation}
    T = n \sum_k \frac{1}{f_k},
\end{equation}
where $f_k$ are the excited frequencies and $n$ is the number of repetitions to average for computing the impedance.
On the other hand, in an experiment with multisine the time required is
\begin{equation}
    T = \frac{1}{f_{min}},
\end{equation}
where $f_min$ is the minimum excited frequency.
Depending on the choice of $n$ and the frequency grid, the stepped method requires 3 to 10 times longer \cite{schoukensBroadbandSteppedSine1999}.
Figure \ref{fig:multisine-length} illustrates this difference by comparing the total acquisition time required for stepped single-sine excitation with different numbers of repetitions to a single multisine period covering the same frequency range.

The impedance at each angular frequency $\omega_k$ is then obtained by isolating the corresponding spectral components of the voltage and current signals through Fourier analysis:
\begin{equation}
Z(\omega_k) = \frac{\mathcal{F}\{v(t)\}(\omega_k)}{\mathcal{F}\{i(t)\}(\omega_k)}.
\end{equation}

The use of multisine excitation was explored as early as the 1970s, notably by Kojima and Fujiwara \cite{kojima1971fourier}, Glover and Smith \cite{gloverAlternatingCurrentPolarography1973}, and Creason and Smith \cite{creasonFourierTransformFaradaic1972}, who demonstrated its advantages in terms of measurement speed and signal-to-noise ratio.
While initially developed for stationary systems, multisine methods naturally lend themselves to applications involving slow system drift.

\subsection{Short-time Fourier transform impedance}

When a non-stationary system drifts slower than the period of the applied multisine, the stationarity assumption is valid over the time window equal to the inverse of the period.
An "instantaneous" impedance can be defined as the ratio between the short-time Fourier transform (denoted $\mathcal{F}$) of the voltage $v(t)$ and current $i(t)$ signals:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:stft}
    Z(\omega, \tau) = \frac{\mathcal{F}[v(t) w(t-\tau)](\omega)}{\mathcal{F}[i(t)w(t-\tau)](\omega)},    
\end{equation}
where $w(t)$ is the window function and $\tau$ is the time resolution of the impedance, equivalent to the window length.
This formulation is schematically illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:stft-scheme}, which shows how a sliding time window is used to extract a local frequency response during a dynamic experiment.

This approach was introduced by Bond, Schwall, and Smith in 1977 \cite{bondOnlineFFTFaradaic1977}, whom used a micro-controller-based online Fourier analysis during cyclic voltammetry experiments, and it is here called short-time Fourier transform (STFT)impedance.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering

    \caption{Principle of short-time Fourier transform (STFT) impedance estimation during a dynamic electrochemical experiment.
    The voltage and current signals are multiplied by a sliding window function, allowing the impedance to be estimated locally in time under the assumption of temporary stationarity. Adapted from \cite{darowickiImpedanceCharacterizationProcess2004}.}
    \label{fig:stft-scheme}
\end{figure}

With the advancement in computing power in the 1990s, Popkirov and Schindler developed a versatile instrumental setup for dynamic impedance spectroscopy, assembling a home-made potentiostat, an arbitrary waveform generator, and a digital acquisition device that streams the multi-frequency voltage and current signals to a personal computer.
This configuration is still used today, and it is how the measurements in this work were carried out.

Various authors have implemented their own versions of this setup in the past three decades using devices from different manufacturers, without changing the basic formula:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hazi and Bond \cite{haziMicrocomputerbasedInstrumentationMultifrequency1997};
    \item Roy, Garland, and Pettit \cite{waltersWeakAdsorptionAnions2001a};
    \item Darowicki and Slepski \cite{darowickiInstantaneousElectrochemicalImpedance2004a};
    \item Schouckens, Pintelon, and Hubin \cite{vangheemElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2004a};
    \item Sacci and Harrington \cite{sacciDynamicImpedanceFormic2009};
    \item Pajkossy and Mészáros \cite{pajkossyConnectionCVsImpedance2020}.
\end{itemize}

What distinguishes the work of different groups is the studied system, the drift speed, and the frequency components of the multisine.
The choice of the frequency range and distribution is important for obtaining the full electrochemical response of the cell while avoiding intermodulation between harmonics and fundamental components.
Intermodulation refers to the phenomenon where two or more different frequency signals interact in a non-linear system, producing additional frequencies that are the sum and difference of the original signals. This leads to errors in the estimation of frequency components amplitudes.

The multisine prepared by Popkirov and Schindler consisted of three decades (100 kHz to 100 Hz, or 0.76 Hz to 0.076 mHz) with 42 prime-number components \cite{popkirovOptimizationPerturbationSignal1993a}.
Roy, Garland, Pettit, and colleagues proposed a multisine with 133 components of only odd frequencies, random phase, and equal amplitude \cite{waltersWeakAdsorptionAnions2001a}.
The choice of the phase and amplitude of each sinusoidal component of the multisine affects the quality of the estimated impedance and it will be discussed in detail in Chapter \ref{ch:acquisition}.
Schouckens, Pintelon, Hubin, and colleagues also proposed a multisine using odd-number harmonics with 220 components between 10 mHz and 2 kHz \cite{vaningelgemAdvantagesOddRandom2009}.
Sacci and Harrington designed a multisine in the frequency band 1 Hz to 1.3 kHz with removed specific harmonics that cause intermodulation, following the work of Popkirov, with amplitude decreasing by a factor of two every two decades.

Table \ref{tab:literature-parameters-stft} highlights that, despite decades of development, STFT-based impedance measurements are typically limited to minimum frequencies on the order of 1~Hz, reflecting the fundamental trade-off between time resolution and low-frequency access.
A detailed chronological review of dynamic impedance spectroscopy work is provided in Appendix \ref{app:literature review stft}.

\section{The limiting lower frequency}

The STFT method is based on the idea that the system is temporary stationary for the time of the window length, but how to determine the lowest frequency that respects the LTI condition?
In the literature have been proposed a couple of formulas that applies to cyclic voltammetry multisine experiments on redox couples\cite{sacciDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2009}\cite{waltersWeakAdsorptionAnions2001}:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{F\nu}{RT} \ll 2\pi f_{min}
\end{equation}
based on the scan rate $\nu$ of the voltage input and 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}i}{\text{d}t} \ll 2\pi A_{min} f_{min}
\end{equation}
based on the derivative of the ouput current $i$, and $A_{min}$ is the amplitude of the system output at frequency $f_{min}$.
These formulas present a general inequality and it is not possible to define how greter the minimum frequency should be compared to the speed of the drift. 
A few time might be sufficient or maybe one or more orders of magnitude is necessary.
Furthermore they don't generalize to other electrochemical systems or other other drift types outside cyclic voltammetry.

The only solution is to proceed by tentative acquiring the impedance at a low desired frequency and test the linearity and stationarity condition via a Kramer-Kroning test.

\section{Dynamic Multi-Frequency Analysis}
\label{sec:dmfa description}

Even when the lowest frequency of the multisine is fast enough to estimate a correct frequency response function, the STFT method has a limitation due to the reciprocity of time and frequency.
In fact, if the window is short, the frequency resolution in the Fourier spectrum is low and it is not possible to identify low frequencies.
The limitation imposed by time-frequency reciprocity is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:frequency-resolution-periods}, which shows how frequency resolution improves only when multiple periods of a signal are sampled.
This problem is worsened by the presence of strong pink noise \sidenote{Pink noise, also known as flicker noise, is a type of electronic noise characterized by a power spectral density that is inversely proportional to frequency, meaning it is stronger at lower frequencies. It commonly occurs in electronic devices due to fluctuations in the properties of materials, impacting the performance of components like transistors and sensors.} in this region as well as the zero-frequency skirt produced by the drift.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = 0.6\textwidth]{figures/deis_state_art/periods and resolution.jpg}
    \caption{Illustration of the time-frequency resolution trade-off.
    Sampling an increasing number of periods improves frequency resolution (red markers) but requires longer observation times, limiting access to low frequencies in non-stationary systems when using time-windowed Fourier analysis.}
    \label{fig:frequency-resolution-periods}
\end{figure}

To increase the frequency resolution, La Mantia, Battistel, and Koster introduced \emph{dynamic multi-frequency analysis} (DMFA) as a method to estimate instantaneous impedance spectra during fast electrochemical experiments while retaining access to low-frequency information \cite{kosterDynamicImpedanceSpectroscopy2017, battistelPhysicalDefinitionDynamic2019}.

The key idea of DMFA is to record the entire multi-frequency signals of the experiment that spans hundreds of periods and perform a \emph{frequency-selective analysis} of the measured signals.
Rather than windowing the signal in time, DMFA isolates individual frequency components in the frequency domain using narrow band-pass filters and reconstructs their time evolution via inverse Fourier transformation.
The time resolution of the instantaneous impedance is no longer determined by the window length, but by the bandwidth of the band-pass filter.

Let $V(\omega)$ and $I(\omega)$ denote the Fourier transforms of the measured voltage and current signals obtained during a dynamic experiment with a multisine excitation.
For a given excitation frequency $\omega_k$, DMFA estimates the instantaneous impedance as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:formual DMFA}
Z(\omega_k, \tau) =
\frac{
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[ V(\omega)\, F(\omega - \omega_k) \right](\tau)
}{
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\!\left[ I(\omega)\, F(\omega - \omega_k) \right](\tau)
},
\end{equation}
where $F(\omega - \omega_k)$ is a flat-top filter function centered at $\omega_k$, $\mathcal{F}^{-1}$ denotes the inverse Fourier transform, and $\tau$ represents time.
This operation extracts the contribution of a single excitation frequency and reconstructs its time-dependent amplitude and phase.
The individual processing steps of the DMFA algorithm are summarized in Figure \ref{fig:dmfa-steps}, highlighting the frequency-selective filtering and reconstruction of time-dependent impedance components.
In Step 3 it is evident that the peaks corresponding on the frequency components are not sharp but broadened: that is the effect of the system's time variance.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width =\textwidth]{figures/deis_state_art/DMFA steps.png}
    \caption{Schematic overview of the dynamic multi-frequency analysis (DMFA) algorithm.
    The full voltage and current spectra are filtered around individual excitation frequencies and transformed back into the time domain to reconstruct the instantaneous impedance.
    Spectral broadening reflects deviations from strict time invariance. Adapted from \cite{piantaEvaluationKineticParameters2023a}.}
    \label{fig:dmfa-steps}
\end{figure}

As filter function the author proposed a symmetric Fermi-Diract function \cite{battistelPhysicalDefinitionDynamic2019}, defined as 
\begin{equation}
g(\omega - \omega_k) =
\frac{[1 + \exp(-n)]^2}
{[1 + \exp(-n(\omega - \omega_k + bw)/bw)]
 [1 + \exp(n(\omega - \omega_k - bw)/bw)]},
\end{equation}
where the parameter $n$ controls the steepness of the filter edges and $bw$ is the bandwidth and its minimum value correspond to the maximum time resolution $\tau$.
The effect of the filter shape on frequency selectivity and temporal smoothing is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:fermi-dirac-filter}.

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{figures/deis_state_art/symmetric fermi dirac.jpg}
    \caption{Symmetric Fermi-Dirac filter used in DMFA.
    (a) Frequency-domain representation for different values of the steepness parameter $n$.
    (b) Corresponding impulse responses in the time domain.
    The filter bandwidth and roll-off control the trade-off between time resolution, frequency isolation, and noise amplification. Adapted from \cite{battistelPhysicalDefinitionDynamic2019}.}
    \label{fig:fermi-dirac-filter}
\end{figure}

The choice of the bandwidth $bw$ and attenuation parameter $n$ play a big role in the quality of the estimated impedance.
In fact, they shape the width and lateral oscillatory behaviour of the equivalent time-domain window \cite{chukwuStatisticalAnalysisMeasurement2022}.
An optimal combinatio of $bw$ and $n$ ensures that two consecutive impedance are indipendent from the information of the other.

When using this type of filter with the DMFA, the resulting impedance dataset soffers of high noise at the extremes beacuse the filter function brings the signal values to zero before the inverse transform is performed.
As a consequnce some of the first and last spectrum of a dataset are always discarded.
Other filter shall be investigated to mitigate this problem.

\subsection{Relation to STFT and interpretation}

From a signal-processing perspective, DMFA can be interpreted as a generalization of the STFT.
When the filter bandwidth is increased to include the full excitation spectrum, the DMFA formulation analytically converges to the STFT-based impedance estimation.
Conversely, narrow filters allow individual frequency components to be tracked independently over time.

In the time domain, the filtering operation corresponds to a convolution of the original signal with the impulse response of the filter, resulting in a smoothed, frequency-specific representation.
This behavior is conceptually related to wavelet transforms, although DMFA is derived directly from Fourier-domain considerations and tailored to multisine excitation.

\section{Best linear time-varying approximation}

While STFT and DMFA rely on signal-processing techniques applied to temporarily stationary data segments, an alternative perspective is to explicitly model the system as linear, but time-varying (LTV).
In 2012 Bruegelmans, Latier and their colleagues proposed the idea of recontructing the best linear time-varying approximation of the output signal of a non-stationary system exposed to multisine perturbation \cite{breugelmansOddRandomPhase2012} and use it to compute the instantanous impedace.

The principle is that a linear time-varying system can be espressed as a sum of $N_p$ parallel connected LTI systems $G_p$ muiltiplied by time-varying gains $b_p(t)$ such that the relation between an input $u(t)$ and output $y(t)$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    y(t) = \sum _{p=0}^{N_p} G_p{u(t)}b_p(t).
\end{equation}

In practice, the BLTVA method consist in performing a parametric fitting of the output Fourer spectrum to approximate the linear and time-invariante response. 
Noise, non-linearities \cite{hallemansNonparametricIdentificationLinear2020, hallemansDetectionClassificationQuantification2021}, and non-stationarities \cite{hallemansBestLinearTimeVarying2021} are estimated form the residues of the fitting.

After the fitting, the instantantneous imepdance is estimated as alwasy as ratio between input and (modelled) output:
\begin{align}\label{eq:bltva_impedance}
    Z_{inst} (\omega_k, \tau) =  \frac{U(\omega_k)}{Y(\omega_k, \tau)} = \frac{U(\omega_k)}{\sum_{p=0}^{N_p} \theta_{p,k}\, b_p(\tau)}, 
\end{align}
where $U$ and $Y$ are Fourier transforms of $u$ and $y$, respectively, and $\theta_{p,k}$ are optimized parameters of the output non-parameteric fitting. 

The group applied the method for the identification of the transfer function of many highly non-stationary electrochemical systems under constant current drift: metal electrodeposition \cite{colletOperandoORPEISStudy2021} \cite{dabirihavighOperandoOddRandom2022}, the conversion of zinc to zirconia \cite{dabirihavighOperandoOddRandom2023}, to study fast charging \cite{hallemansOperandoElectrochemicalImpedance2022} and SEI formation in Li-ion batteries \cite{dabirihavighOperandoORPEISMonitoring2025}.

\subsection{Comparison with DMFA}
The BLTVA estimation is simalr to the DMFA in the sense that the voltage and current signals are Fourier transformed in its entirety before the fitting.
Furthermore the fitting is performed in the frequency band ($\{\omega_{k-1}, \omega_k, \omega_{k+1}\}$) which is equivalent to a rectangular band-pass filter.

The advantage of the BLTVA is that the resulting impedance is LTI by definition and doens't require a validation, but its excecution is computationally more expensive because it requires an iterative regression procedure.

Table \ref{tab:literature-parameters-dmfa-bltva} emphasizes that DMFA and BLTVA address complementary regions of the non-stationary impedance problem, trading computational complexity for access to lower frequencies under stronger drift conditions.
A complete comparison is not possible since the drift and frequency ranges are so different.

An interesting work would be to perform all the methods for impedance estimation on the same simulated data under different condition to uncover the effect of the drift speed to the stationarity and how the experietnal methods approeach the ideal theoretical impedance.
