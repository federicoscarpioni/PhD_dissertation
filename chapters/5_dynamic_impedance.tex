\chapter{Non-stationary Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy: State of the Art}

\section{Evolution of Multi-Frequency EIS}

\subsection{From Single Sine to Multi-Sine (1970s-1990s)}

Traditional EIS relied on stepped-sine measurements where each frequency is measured sequentially using lock-in amplifiers or frequency response analyzers (FRAs). While precise, this approach requires 30 minutes to several hours for a full spectrum. The advent of microcomputers and Fast Fourier Transform algorithms in the 1970s opened the possibility of multi-sine excitation—simultaneously applying multiple frequencies and extracting impedance from a single measurement \cite{kojima1971fourier, creasonFourierTransformFaradaic1972, gloverAlternatingCurrentPolarography1973}.

Bond, Schwall and Smith (1977) pioneered the coupling of multi-sine with cyclic voltammetry \cite{schwallOnlineFastFourier1977, bondOnlineFFTFaradaic1977}, creating the first "dynamic impedance" measurement—later termed Fourier Transform Faradaic Admittance Measurements (FT-FAM). Despite early promise, the method remained niche due to hardware complexity and the dominance of FRA-based instruments.

The breakthrough came with Popkirov and Schindler (1992), who developed a complete FFT-based impedance spectrometer using a custom potentiostat, waveform generator, and data acquisition system \cite{popkirovOptimizationPerturbationSignal1993a}. Their work established key principles: multi-sine phase optimization to minimize crest factor \cite{popkirovOptimizationPerturbationSignal1993a}, validation against nonlinear distortions \cite{popkirovValidationExperimentalData1993a}, and demonstration across diverse applications (electropolymerization, metal dissolution, redox kinetics). This work defined the instrumental architecture still used today.

\subsection{Operando Impedance with Short-Time Fourier Transform (2000s-2010s)}

Three major research groups expanded multi-sine EIS in the 2000s, each with distinct focuses:

\textbf{Roy and coworkers (Clarkson University)} applied multi-sine EIS to surface electrochemistry—adsorption kinetics, metal electrodeposition, and electrochemical planarization—using commercial instruments controlled via LabVIEW and analyzing data with short-time Fourier transforms \cite{waltersWeakAdsorptionAnions2001a, garlandAnalysisExperimentalConstraints2004}. Their work established practical guidelines for parameter selection and experimental limitations.

\textbf{Darowicki and Ślepski (Gdańsk)} focused on corrosion science and energy storage, developing both chirp and multi-sine perturbations \cite{darowickiDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2003a, darowickiInstantaneousElectrochemicalImpedance2004a}. They demonstrated applications ranging from stress corrosion cracking to fuel cells and redox flow batteries, extending frequency ranges down to 30 mHz.

\textbf{Hubin, Pintelon, Schoukens (Brussels/VUB)} brought rigorous system identification theory to electrochemical impedance. Their "Odd Random Phase" (ORP) multi-sine approach \cite{vaningelgemAdvantagesOddRandom2009} separated nonlinear distortions from linear impedance by using only odd harmonics, enabling quantitative validation of data quality. They provided systematic comparisons of excitation signals \cite{schoukensSurveyExcitationSignals1988a} and established that multi-sine methods achieve superior signal-to-noise ratios compared to sequential measurements \cite{schoukensBroadbandSteppedSine1999}.

These groups established multi-sine EIS for quasi-stationary systems where parameter drift is slow relative to the measurement period (typically >10 seconds for one multi-sine cycle).

\subsection{Non-Stationary System Identification (2012-Present)}

The limitation of STFT-based methods is the assumption of local stationarity: the impedance must not change significantly during the window length (typically one multi-sine period). This restricts measurements to either high frequencies (>1 Hz) or systems with very slow drift.

\textbf{Bruegelmans and colleagues (VUB/UAntwerp)} extended system identification theory for truly time-varying systems \cite{breugelmansOddRandomPhase2012}, developing the Best Linear Time-Varying Approximation (BLTVA) framework. Their method quantifies both nonlinearity and non-stationarity \cite{hallemansDetectionClassificationQuantification2021, hallemansBestLinearTimeVarying2021} and has been applied to fast electrochemical processes: electrograining, metal electrodeposition, and SEI formation in batteries \cite{dabirihavighOperandoORPEISMonitoring2025, hallemansOperandoElectrochemicalImpedance2022}.

\textbf{Battistel, La Mantia and colleagues (Bremen)} developed Dynamic Multi-Frequency Analysis (DMFA) \cite{kosterDynamicImpedanceSpectroscopy2017, battistelPhysicalDefinitionDynamic2019}, using frequency-domain filtering rather than time-domain windowing. By convolving multi-sine data with a narrow-band filter (e.g., Fermi-Dirac function), they extract localized impedance spectra even under strong drift. DMFA has been applied to intercalation processes \cite{scarpioniElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2023a, erinmwingbovoDynamicImpedanceSpectroscopy2019, erinmwingbovoDynamicImpedanceSpectroscopy2020} and dissolution reactions \cite{kosterMeasurementAnalysisDynamic2018}. The group also addressed the challenge of fitting large time-resolved datasets through regularized optimization \cite{battistelAnalysisNonstationaryImpedance2016, chukwuDynamicImpedanceSpectroscopy2023}.

Recent work by Zappen et al. \cite{zappenApplicationTimeResolvedMultiSine2018} demonstrated battery-specific applications, including fast impedance screening for quality control and operando measurements during thermal runaway \cite{zappenInOperandoImpedanceSpectroscopy2020a}.

\section{Remaining Challenges}

Despite these advances, several limitations persist:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Broadband coverage}: Most studies focus on narrow frequency bands (1 Hz - 10 kHz) due to data management challenges at high sampling rates.
\item \textbf{Long-term measurements}: Continuous operando impedance over battery lifetime (weeks to months) requires automated systems and online data processing—capabilities not demonstrated in prior work.
\item \textbf{Accessibility}: Existing implementations rely on custom software or expensive FPGA-based systems, limiting adoption by the broader electrochemistry community.
\end{itemize}

This thesis addresses these gaps by developing an open-source, automated methodology for broadband (10 mHz - 100 kHz) impedance acquisition during multi-week battery cycling, validated across multiple electrochemical systems.
