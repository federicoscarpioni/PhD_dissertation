The estimation of electrochemical impedance under non-stationary conditions requires not only appropriate theoretical models and signal processing methods, but also an experimental platform with the flexibility to implement user-designed excitations and cell signal measurements at high frequency rates.
Conventional impedance analyzers are designed for stationary systems and sequential single-frequency excitation.
All data processing is performed in the background and the user receives directly the resulting impedance.
In dynamic impedance spectroscopy, the excitation waveform, acquisition strategy, and timing control directly affect the validity of the estimated frequency response.
Therefore, the iInstrumentation must be designed for the specific identification and signal processing methodology.

This chapter presents the hardware and software architecture developed to perform continuous, broadband impedance measurements under controlled operating conditions.
Emphasis is placed on waveform generation, synchronized acquisition, and automation, which together enable long-duration experiments, such as the study of impedance evolution during battery aging.

\section{Electrochemical instrumentation}

In Chapter \ref{ch:deis state art}, I stated that the typical experimental setup for dynamic impedance consists of a potentiostat/galvanostat (or only potentiostat for short), an arbitrary waveform generator, and a digital acquisition device.
While the latter provides high-fidelity signal generation and sampling, the core of the setup is the potentiostat, which applies those signals and controls the cell terminals.
The potentiostat provides the user with built-in perturbation waveforms used in cyclic voltammetry, potentiostatic, and galvanostatic experiments.
The same hardware is used for classic electrochemical experiments and for multi-frequency ones; it is important to discuss the inner components of a typical instrument to address how the setup was implemented for this work.

\subsection{Generalities of potentiostats}
Electrochemical methods consist of a perturbation of one cell variable among voltage and current while recording the other as function of time.
To do so, two types of instruments are needed: a potentiostat or a galvanostat; usually packed inside one banchtop device.
The purpose of a potentiostat is to maintain the potential of a working electrode (WE) at a prescribed value relative to a reference electrode (RE). 
This is achieved using a high-gain feedback loop. 
The potential difference between WE and RE is measured through a high-impedance \footnote{1-10 $T\Omega$} voltage follower connected to the RE. 
This element ensures that only a negligible current flows through the RE, preserving its equilibrium potential. 
The measured potential is compared to a user-defined setpoint, and the resulting error signal drives a power amplifier connected to the counter electrode (CE). 
By adjusting the CE potential, the amplifier forces the WE potential toward the setpoint.
The output stage must source or sink the full cell current while maintaining stability of the feedback loop.

This check is performed continuously with a velocity dictated by the bandwidth of the operational amplifier.
Usually, this value is arbitrary on a scale of integer values.
High values mean fast loops, while low values result in slower loops.
To reduce noise or fluctuation a low bandwidth is an ideal choice while for measuring the dynamic of fast systems is necessary to use high values and introduce more noise in the sampling.

The current flowing through the WE-CE path is measured by a current-to-voltage converter, typically implemented using a transimpedance amplifier. The converter's output provides a low-noise measurement of the cell current and is digitised for acquisition and further processing. The transimpedance gain determines the current range, and switching networks or programmable gain amplifiers are often used to accommodate multiple current's order of magnitude.

In galvanostatic mode, the instrument controls the current rather than the potential. 
The measured current is compared to a desired setpoint, and the feedback loop adjusts the CE potential to maintain the prescribed current. The WE potential freely responds to the applied current and is measured against the RE potential. Internally, the same analog electronic blocks are used as in potentiostatic operation, but with the controll loop comparing the setpoint current and the cell current (WE-CE).

Because the implementation of dynamic impedance spectroscopy relies on injecting external perturbations and accessing raw analogue signals, it is essential to understand the internal structure of a potentiostat and its control loop.

\subsection{Auxiliary analogue inputs and outputs}

Some modern commercial potentiostats incorporate auxiliary input and output connections to enable external excitation or synchronization with additional hardware.
Analog input ports are typically connected upstream of the main control loop, allowing externally generated waveforms to be superimposed on the programmed potential or current (see Figure \ref{fig:pot-galv-schematics}, top left of the schematic).
This functionality is essential for experiments requiring arbitrary perturbations and high-speed signal recording.

Auxiliary analog inputs provide direct access to measured analog signals before digital conversion, bypassing part of the instrument's internal filtering and signal conditioning.
Recording these signals enables high-resolution acquisition beyond the default sampling configuration of the potentiostat with a dedicated device.

\subsection{Digital control and communication}

Although the core functionality of the potentiostat is analog circuitry, the control and signal acquisition is digital and implemented with field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs\footnote{In FPGAs, logic blocks can be programmed to execute simple digital operations like sums and multiplication. The advantage is the possibility of parallelizing these operations for real-time data processing. They are more expensive than microcontrollers}) or micro-controllers.
Each channel of the potentiostat is connected with a communication board that sends signals and receives commands to and from a personal computer via USB or Ethernet.
This interface sets on the hardware the measurement parameters chosen by the user and retrieves the sampled data.
The instrument is then capable of working independently of the computer, storing data temporarily in a buffer memory.

\section{Multi-sine design}
\label{sec:multisine}
A multisine is the superposition of sinusoidal components:
\[
u(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_k \sin(j2\pi f_k t + \phi_k).
\]
It allows simultaneous excitation of a large set of frequencies with a single time-domain waveform, reducing the experiment duration by at least a third in battery settings.

Obtaining high-quality instantaneous impedance in non-stationary conditions, however, requires careful design of the multisine: frequency grid, crest-factor minimization, amplitude choice and memory constraints of the AWG.

\subsection{Frequencies selection}

The frequencies $f$ must be chosen such that one period of the whole multisine contains integer numbers of oscillations to avoid spectral leakage due to non-periodicity.
The period is then defined by one oscillation of the sine wave with lowest frequency: $T = 1/f_{min}$.
Every other frequency must satisfy the condition $f_k = m \times f_{min}$, where $m$ is an integer. 
In this work, we generate the desired frequencies from a harmonic series of integers to ensure the above condition and the possibility to reuse the sequence for multisines with different $f_{min}$.
With the term harmonic sequence, I refer to $h_k = f_k/f_{min}$; such that it always starts from 1 and must contain only integers.
The sequence is generated as a logarithmic distribution with a number of points per decade between six and ten.
Lower values give sparse spectra, while high values give denser ones.
Denser spectra are more beneficial for data regression, having more points to fit in the cost function, but the signal power increases.
For this work, I obtained sufficient impedance density with eight points per decade. 

\subsection{Amplitude optimization}
\label{sec:amplitude optim}
When the amplitudes are all the same, the multisine is said to be "flat". The question is: how to decide the amplitudes for best signal-to-noise result? It can be achieved via analytical formulas or experimental extrapolation.
The first case was used in Battistel \cite{battistelAnalysisNonstationaryImpedance2016} and Hallemans \cite{hallemansElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2023}, with opposite results, making the amplitudes drop logarithmically with increasing or decreasing frequency.

In this work, I followed an empirical strategy based on the impedance of previous experiments.
One can simply measure a classic stepped-sine impedance with any and use its modulus to calculate the input's relative amplitudes to obtain a desired ouput's relative amplitudes.
Alternatively, and more effective for energy storage systems, is to measure the non-stationary impedance first using a flat multisine and then extract from the often-noisy data the average value and use its modulus.
In mathematical terms, being $|Z_{test}(\omega)|_{avg}$ the average of the impedance modulus, the desired output's voltage for the frequency component $\omega_k$ is $v_{des}(\omega_k)$; hence, the current amplitude follows Ohm's law as:
\begin{equation}
i(\omega_k) = \frac{v_{des}}{|Z_{test}(\omega_k)|_{avg}}.
\end{equation}

The output of the waveform generator is olways a voltage and it is converted to current by the potentiostat during the chronoamperometry technique using the active measurement resistor $R_m$ placed in series to the working electrode (ref. Figure \ref{fig:pot-galv-schematics}).
In the instrument, the value of such resistor is dictated by the choise of the current range.
For the experiment in this work, I always chose the same current range of 10 mA, which translates to a measurement resistor of 100 Ohms.
Hence, for the galvanostatic step, the actual peak-to-peak voltage of the generated waveform is 
\begin{equation}
V(\omega_k) = R_m \times i(\omega_k).
\end{equation}

For the potentiostatic step, instead, the amplitude of the multisine is 
\begin{equation}
V(\omega_k) = |Z_{test}(\omega_k)|_{avg}.
\end{equation}

Despite sounding just like a technicality, the choice of the relative amplitude is a difficult engeneering problem beacuse is directly connect with the signal-to-noise ratio, hence the quality of the estimated impedance.

\subsection{Phase optimization}
The process of identification should be carried out with small-amplitude excitation around the operating point.
Multisine waveforms can often have large peak-to-peak total amplitude after optimizing the relative amplitudes, which derive from the superposition of multiple sinusoidal components at the value of their peaks.
In most cases, however, it can be reduced by acting on the phase of each sinusoidal component in the total multisine.
The quantity of interest to reduce is the \textit{crest factor} (CF), defined as

$$
CF = \frac{|x_{max}|}{x_{rms}}
$$

where $x_{rms}$ is the root-mean-square, and consequently the peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR) equals the square of the crest factor.
The crest factor of a pure sinusoidal wave is $\sqrt{2}$, which serves as a lower ideal boundary (it is matched only by a chirp waveform; a square wave, however, has a CF of 1). 
 
In this work, iterative-stochastic optimization was used in all the experiments presented later; although not optimal, it gives a sufficiently low CF for the exploratory stage of this work.
The method consists of sampling the phases from a randomly generated distribution and computing the crest factor.
The process is repeated a certain number of times (e.g., 50 or 100) and the phase that produces the lowest crest factor is kept.
A summary of the main methods for crest-factor minimization present in the literature is described in Appendix \ref{ch:crest factor}.

\subsection{AWG memory limitations and splitting strategy}
\label{sec:multisine split}
Benchtop waveform generators consist of electronic circuits constructed around a digital-to-analog converter (DAC).
An integrated modern DAC chip has a buffer memory in which the waveform values are written, and during generation, such memory is read in a loop (called look-up table generation).
Commonly, commercial devices have a buffer memory consisting of 12 to 32 kSa, which is quite small for the excitations used in batteries that might span 6--7 decades (at least at the laboratory scale).
A solution used in digital signal processing devices (used, for example, for the playback of audio files in any electronic device) is called direct digital synthesis (DDS), which creates direct communication between a DAC and a micro-controller memory.
This option requires proficiency in the programming and setting of micro-controllers, specifically synchronizing the components to the same clock. 

In our laboratory, we use instead a dedicated benchtop Arbitrary Waveform Generator (AWG) with two channels, each with 64 MSa; a huge upgrade from conventional integrated DACs.
This amount of memory allows multisine waveforms containing at least 6 decades.
To study batteries, however, my goal was to have seven or more decades of frequency range in the multisine and to be able to switch between two waveforms optimized specifically for chronopotentiometry and chronoamperometry.
The solution I developed is to divide a multisine waveform into two waveforms and use the ability of the generator to sum the signals of the two channels ("dual channel mode") at the output.
The two separate waves have fewer samples, and the two types of multisines can easily fit in the memory of the device.

As an example, let's consider a seven-decade multisine waveform from 10 mHz to 100 kHz, generated at 1 MHz.
As generation frequency, using 10 times the value of the maximum frequency multisine component gives good results, although the literature advises using even 100 times to avoid spectral leakage.
Keysight devices have a proprietary "TrueForm" technology to reduce jitter that might help limit spectral leakage in the final signal.

Generating one single broadband waveform produces a file of 100 MSa (obtained as $period \times f_{gen}$).
Dividing the frequency band at the value of 100 Hz creates two smaller multisines: one from 10 mHz to 100 Hz (not included) generated at 1000 Hz (size 100 kSa), and a second one from exactly 100 Hz to 100 kHz generated at 1 MHz, which, choosing a period of 1 s, leads to a waveform of 100 kSa.
The two smaller multisines take up only 200 kSa of the generator memory to produce the same excitation as the complete multisine of 100 MSa.
Figure \ref{fig:multisine-split-time-domain} reports the original and reduced multisines in the time domain.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = \textwidth]{figures/acquisition/multisine_split.png}
    \caption{Broadband multisine generation using frequency-band splitting.
    A single wideband multisine (right) is reproduced by summing two narrower-band multisines (left), each fitting within the memory constraints of the waveform generator.
    This strategy enables excitation spanning multiple decades of frequency without loss of spectral coherence.}
    \label{fig:multisine-split-time-domain}
\end{figure}

In the example, I used two arbitrary parameters: the 100 Hz boundary frequency and the 1 s period of the high-band waveform.
For my case, 100 Hz sits in the middle; this might be adjusted case by case with the same rationale.
Regarding the period of the second wave, one might think of using 0.01 seconds to have one exact period of the lowest frequency, but it must be considered that the frequencies of the two waves should follow the same rules described before to avoid intermodulation artifacts; being made from a sequence of harmonics.
Logarithmic sequences optimized for intermodulation tend to be sparser on the low-frequency part and denser at higher frequencies.
If for both waveforms one generates the frequencies starting from the harmonic of 1, the measured impedance spectra would have a sparser point distribution in the decade from 100 Hz to 1 kHz, where the high-band multisine waveform starts.
See Figure \ref{fig:splitting-design} second row.

To get a uniform distribution of points, I generated the high-frequency series starting from 1 Hz and discarded the frequencies until 100 Hz (not included), since they are already present in the low-band waveform.
Then, the period of the waveform must be 1 Hz; otherwise, there will be spectral leakage in the final excitation (note that the frequencies of the high-frequency series are all integer multiples of 1 Hz).
The sum of the two shorter waveforms generated with this method has the exact distribution of a single-generated wave as reported in the bottom part of Figure \ref{fig:splitting-design}.
The effect of different strategies for restarting harmonic sequences when splitting a broadband multisine is illustrated in the same Figure \ref{fig:splitting-design}.

\begin{figure}[h]

To summarize, for a CC-CV battery experiments, one channel of the generator has in memory two low-band multisine waves, one optimized for chronoamperometry and the other for chronopotentiometry, and the other channel would have loaded the two optimized high-band multisine waves.

\section{Automation software}
\label{sec:software}

\subsection{Problem statement}
Dynamic impedance spectroscopy measurements performed during battery aging experiments extend over time scales of days to weeks and generate continuous, high-bandwidth voltage and current data streams. When broadband excitation is applied throughout cycling, the resulting acquisition rate rapidly exceeds the available volatile memory of standard data acquisition systems. As a consequence, the feasibility of long-duration non-stationary impedance experiments is not limited by electrochemical stability, but by the ability to sustain uninterrupted signal acquisition and storage over arbitrarily long time horizons.

In conventional laboratory workflows, data acquisition is often constrained to fixed-length recordings whose duration is dictated by the available RAM of the acquisition device or host computer. This approach is incompatible with aging studies, where impedance must be tracked continuously across hundreds of electrochemical techniques and repeated charge-discharge cycles. Without an automated strategy for segmenting, storing, and reinitializing acquisition buffers, dynamic impedance measurements would be restricted to short snapshots, preventing systematic observation of slow degradation phenomena.

For this reason, the automation in the present work is conceived primarily as a memory-management and scalability mechanism. The software framework developed here enables the acquisition of multi-frequency signals over arbitrarily long experiments by periodically transferring voltage and current data blocks from volatile memory to persistent storage, while maintaining phase coherence and continuity of the excitation. 
% This approach allows non-stationary impedance spectroscopy to be embedded seamlessly into long-term cycling protocols, making it possible to observe impedance evolution as a function of state of charge and state of health without imposing artificial limits on experiment duration.

% Before I joined the ESECS group, the acquisition of multi-frequency cell signals was performed by manually setting the three instruments independently and streaming the signal from the acquisition device to the computer with a procedural MATLAB script calling the PicoTech SDK.
% This approach binds the maximum signal length to the computer RAM because saving directly to disk is not possible due to the slow speed of writing to mechanical hard drives compared to the high streaming frequency.

\subsection{Software solution}
A control loop must monitor the sequence advancement and loop number returned by querying the potentiostat communication board.
BioLogic EC-Lab or EC-Lab Express do not allow automating this process. The output triggers functionality is too limited for fine tuning the acquisition as described here.
Luckily, BioLogic provides a simple software development kit (SDK) to communicate with the potentiostats using various programming languages, which I expanded into a more sophisticated object-oriented Python library that I called \verb|pyeclab|\footnote{Available at https://github.com/federicoscarpioni/pyeclab}.

Object-oriented programming (OOP) is a software design paradigm in which complex systems are represented as collections of interacting \emph{objects}.  
Each object encapsulates both data and the operations that can be performed on that data, allowing physical devices or logical processes to be represented as modular software entities. 
Tipycally, objects are schematized as in Figure \ref{fig:generic_object} to highlight their name, attributes and methods.
For semplicity, in the rest of this disussion, only the methods (that incapsulate the behaviour) are represented.

In the present implementation, each laboratory instrument (potentiostat, waveform generator, and oscilloscope) is abstracted as an independent object exposing a well-defined \emph{interface} for configuration, status monitoring, and data exchange.  
This abstraction layer separates the experimental logic from the hardware-specific communication details, enabling the same high-level control code to operate across different hardware configurations with minimal modification.

Encapsulation and modularity are particularly advantageous for long-duration experiments, where robustness and maintainability are essential.  
By structuring the automation software as a set of interacting objects, individual components can be extended, replaced, or debugged without affecting the entire acquisition framework.  
This design also enables \emph{hierarchical composition}, where higher-level objects coordinate the behaviour of lower-level instrument objects, reflecting the physical structure of the experimental setup.

Through the \verb|Channel| object, the status and data points are recursively retrieved from each channel of the potentiostat to the computer program, which converts, stores, and plots data, and executes a \emph{callback} function when a technique is finished.

The coordination of the electrochemical sequence, excitation waveform, and data acquisition is implemented through a central software control loop.
This loop continuously monitors the execution state of the potentiostat, synchronizes the activation of the multisine excitation, and manages the transfer of acquired data from volatile memory to persistent storage.
By reacting to technique completion events and loop counters, the control logic ensures uninterrupted acquisition while preserving phase coherence between excitation and response signals.

Because the potentiostat, waveform generator, and acquisition device operate simultaneously and exchange data in real time, the automation software must manage multiple processes concurrently.  
Sequential execution of instrument commands would introduce delays and risk data loss, particularly during high-bandwidth streaming.  
For this reason, the control software employs concurrent execution through \emph{multithreading}.

In multithreaded programming, multiple execution threads run within the same process and share access to memory and program state.  
This approach allows independent tasks to proceed in parallel while remaining coordinated through a central control logic.  

To make the automation possible, each instrument must independently execute its routine in a dedicated thread, with its actions being triggered from the main control loop.
The typical approach is to implement each instrument as a \emph{worker} object, which executes operations inside a \emph{while: True} loop \footnote{It always contains a pause during which the main thread is allowed to execute code for another worker object while new data are accumulated in another.}.
The multi-threading standard library of Python, \verb|threading|, is capable of automatically managing all objects without user indication in the code (compared to other libraries like \verb|asyncio|).

A worker object has a property with the name \verb|running| that can be used by other objects to check their status.
This idea is the basis of all the libraries used to synchronize the potentiostat, waveform generator, and acquisition device.
This shared-state approach allows the main control loop to coordinate the execution of all instruments without blocking, ensuring real-time synchronization between electrochemical control, excitation, and data acquisition.

The automatic switch of the multisine between galvannostatic and potentiostatic electrochemical experiments (see Figure \ref{fig:python-controll-loop} the AWG timeline) can still be done by \verb|DEISChannel| for the case of combined multisine waves, passing the \verb|MultisineWaveCombined| object, also provided in the package \verb|TrueFormAWG|, during initialization.
To allow effective communication with the waveform generation suring multisine updates between galvanostatic and potentiostatic techniques a 3~s open circuit period is added in the sequence. 

The multithreaded capability is not only used to coordinate the three instruments for a dynamic impedance spectroscopy, but also allows to use multiple channels of the potentiostat (and relative waveforms generators and acquisition devices) indipendently and simultanously.

\section{Summary and Contribution}

This chapter presents the experimental framework developed for the acquisition of multi-frequency signlas for the estimation of instantnaous impednace in non-stationary conditions. The focus was deliberately placed on the hardware integration, excitation design, sampling strategy, and software automation required to enable dynamic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy under realistic battery operating conditions.

A central technical contribution of this work is the development of a strategy to generate long, arbitrary broadband excitation signals using commercial laboratory instrumentation.
By exploiting the dual-channel capabilities and large onboard memory of modern arbitrary waveform generators, and by introducing a frequency-subdivision approach, it was possible to synthesize multisine excitations spanning up to seven decades of frequency while preserving spectral coherence and avoiding intermodulation artifacts.
This capability exceeds what is typically achievable with conventional embedded DAC architectures and is essential for broadband impedance characterization of electrochemical systems.

Equally important is the automation layer developed to coordinate the potentiostat, waveform generator, and high-speed acquisition device over long-duration cycling experiments.
A modular, open-source software ecosystem was created to manage instrument synchronization, multisine activation, continuous data streaming, buffering, and metadata logging without user intervention.
This infrastructure enables uninterrupted acquisition of multi-frequency voltage and current signals over many charge--discharge cycles, a prerequisite for studying impedance evolution during battery aging.
The ability to reliably acquire long, synchronized, broadband datasets under controlled electrochemical conditions was a limiting factor in dynamic impedance spectroscopy up to this point.
This work establishes a practical and extensible platform for time-resolved impedance analysis in batteries and other electrochemical systems.
