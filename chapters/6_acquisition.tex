The estimation of electrochemical impedance under non-stationary conditions requires not only appropriate theoretical models and signal processing methods, but also experimental an experimental platform with the flexibility of implementing user deisgned excitation and sample cell signals at high frequency rates.
Conventional impedance analyzers are designed for stationary systems and sequential single-frequency excitation.
All of the data processing is preformed in the backgroun and the user recieves directly the resulting impedance.
In dynamic impedance spectroscopy, the excitation waveform, acquisition strategy, and timing control directly affect the validity of the estimated frequency response.
Instrumentation therefore cannot be treated as a black box, but must be designed for the specific identification and signal processing methodology.

This chapter presents the hardware and software architecture developed to perform continuous, broadband impedance measurements under controlled operating conditions.
Emphasis is placed on waveform generation, synchronized acquisition, and automation, which together enable long-duration experiments, such as the study of impedance evolution during battery aging.

\section{Electrochemical instrumentation}

In Chapter \ref{ch:deis state art} I stated that the typical experimental setup for dynamic impedance is constituted of a potentiostat/galvanostat (or only potentiostat for short), an arbitrary waveform generator and a digital acquisition device.
While the letter provides high fidelty signals generation and sampling the core of the setup is the potentiostat, which applies those signals and controls the cell terminals.
The potentiostat provides the user with built-in perurbation waveform used in cyclic voltammetry, potentiostatic and galvanostatic experiment. 
The same hardware is used for classic electrochemical experiment and for multi-frequency ones so it is important to discuss the inner components of a typical instrument to adress later how the setup was implemented for this work.

\subsection{Generalities of potentiostats}
Electrochemical methods consist of a perturbation of one cell variable among voltage and current while recording the other as function of time.
To do so, two types of instruments are needed: a potentiostat or a galvanostat; usually packed inside one banchtop device.
The purpose of a potentiostat is to maintain the potential of a working electrode (WE) at a prescribed value relative to a reference electrode (RE). 
This is achieved using a high-gain feedback loop. 
The potential difference between WE and RE is measured through a high-impedance voltage follower connected to the RE. 
This element ensures that only a negligible current flows through the RE, preserving its equilibrium potential. 
The measured potential is compared to a user-defined setpoint, and the resulting error signal drives a power amplifier connected to the counter electrode (CE). 
By adjusting the CE potential, the amplifier forces the WE potential toward the setpoint.
The output stage must source or sink the full cell current while maintaining stability of the feedback loop.

This check is performed continously with a velocity dictated by the bandwidth of the operational amplifier. 
Usually this value is arbitrary in a scale of integer.
Hihg values means fast loops, while low values, lower.
To reduce noise or fluctuation a low bandwidth is an ideal choice while for measuring the dynamic of fast system is necessary to use high values and introduce more noise.

The current flowing through the WE-CE path is measured by a current-to-voltage converter, typically implemented using a transimpedance amplifier. The converter output provides a low-noise measurement of the cell current and is digitised for acquisition and further processing. The transimpedance gain determines the current range, and switching networks or programmable gain amplifiers are often used to accommodate multiple decades of current.

In galvanostatic mode, the instrument controls the current rather than the potential. 
The measured current is compared to a desired setpoint, and the feedback loop adjusts the CE potential to maintain the prescribed current. The WE potential freely responds to the applied current and is measured through the high-impedance RE input. Internally, the same analogue blocks are used as in potentiostatic operation, but with the error signal derived from the difference between measured and commanded current. High-quality galvanostatic control requires wide bandwidth and accurate transimpedance conversion, as well as protection circuitry to prevent saturation when the cell dynamics change abruptly.

\subsection{Auxiliary analogue inputs and outputs}

Some of the modern commercial potentiostats incorporate auxiliary input and output connections to enable external excitation or synchronisation with additional hardware. 
Analogue input ports are typically connected upstream of the main control loop, allowing externally generated waveforms to be superimposed on the programmed potential or current (see \ref{fig:pot/galv schematics} b) top left of the schematics). 
Depending on the instrument architecture, the auxiliary output is added either at the setpoint input of the control amplifier or directly at the summing node of the error amplifier. 
This functionality is essential for experiments requiring composite perturbations as will be described in the Methods part of this work.

Auxiliary analogue inputs provide direct access to the measured analogue signals before digital conversion bypassing part of the instrument's internal filtering and signal conditioning. 
Recording these signals enables high-resolution acquisition beyond the default sampling configuration of the potentiostat with a dedicated device.

\section{Multi-sine design}
\label{sec:multisine}
A multisine is the superposition of sinusoidal components:
\[
u(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} A_k \sin(j2\pi f_k t + \phi_k).
\]
It allows the simultaneous excitation of a large set of frequencies with a single time-domain waveform, reducing the experiment duration of at least a third in battery settings.

Obtaining high-quality non-stationary impedance, however, requires careful design of the multisine: frequency grid, crest-factor minimization, amplitude choice and memory constraints of the AWG.

\subsection{Frequencies selection}

The frequencies $f$ must be chosen in a way that one period of the whole multi-sine contains integer numbers of oscillation to ovoid spectral leakage due to non-periodicity. 
The period is then defined by one oscillation of the sinus with lowest frequency $T=\frac{1}{f_{min}}$.
Every other frequency must satisfy the condition $f_k=m\times f_{min}$, where k is an integer. 
In this work we generate the desired frequencies from an harmonic series of integer to ensure the above condition and the possibility to re-use the sequence for multi-sine with different $f_{min}$. 
With the term harmonic sequence I refer to $h_k =\frac{f_k}{f_{min}}$; such that it always starts from 1 and must contain only integers. The sequence is generated as a logarithmic distribution with a number of point per decade between six and ten. 
Lower values gives spares spectra while high values give a denser one. 
Denser spectra are more beneficial for the data regression having more points to cast in the cost function, but the power of the signal increases. 
For this work I obtained a sufficient impedance density with eigth ponints per decade. 

\subsection{Amplitude optimization}
\label{sec:amplitude optim}
When the amplitudes are all the same, it is said to have a "flat" multi-sine. 
To question is: how to decide the amplitudes?  It can be achieved via analytical formulas or experimental extrapolation. 
The first case was used in Battistel and Hellemans, with opposite results, making the amplitudes drop logarithmically with the increase of the frequency.  
In my work, I measured the non-stationary impedance for many different chemistry and geometries obtaining quite different results in sense of the module of the impedance. 
Hence, I prefer the approach of choosing the amplitudes based on previous experiment. 
One can just measure a classic stepped-sine impedance with any electrochemical workstation and use its module to calculate the desired input amplitude. 
Alternatively, and more effective for energy storage systems, is to measure the non-stationary impedance firs using a flat multi-sine and then extract from the, often noisy, data the average value and use again its module.
% Moved from the other chapter
Firstly I measured a complete cycle of the battery with dynamic impedance spectroscopy using a equal amplitudes on a test cell (not part of the dataset), and then I used the avarage of the module of such impednace and used it to calculate the amplitudes for the input in order to obtain an output with equal magnitude for each frequencies. 
In mathematical therms, being $|Z_{test}(\omega)|_{avg}$ the avarege of the impedance module, the desired output voltage is unitary for all frequencies, enche the current amplitude follows Ohm's law as:

$$
i(\omega_k) = \frac{1}{|Z_{test}(\omega_k)|_{avg}}.
$$

The output of the waveform generator is a voltage and it is converted to current by the electrochemical workstation during the chronoamperometry technique using the active measurement resistor ($R_m$). 
For this experiments I choose always the same current range of 10 mA for the sequence that translates to a measurement resistor of 100 Ohms. 
Hence for the galvanostatic step the actual peak-to-peak voltage of the generated waveform is 

$$
V(\omega_k) = R_m \times i(\omega_k).
$$

For the potentiostatic step instead the amplitude of the multisine is 

$$
V(\omega_k) = |Z_{test}(\omega_k)|_{avg}.
$$

\subsection{Phase optimization}
The process of identification should be carried out with small amplitude excitation around the operating point point. 
Multi-sine waveforms can often time have large peak to peak total amplitude after optimizing the relative amplitudes derives by the superimposition of multiple sinusoidal component at the value of their peak. 
In most cases though, it can be reduced acting on the phase of each sinusoidal component in the total multisine. 
The quantity one is interested to reduce is the \textit{crest factor} (CF) defined as

$$
CF = \frac{|x_{max}|}{x_{rms}}
$$

where $x_{rms}$ is the root-mean-square and consequently the peak-to-average power factor (PAPR) equal to the square of the crest factor. 
The crest factor of a pure sinusoidal wave if $\sqrt{2}$ that serves as a lower ideal boundary  (it is matched only by a chirp waveform, a square wave though as a CF of 1). 
 
In this work, the iterative-stochastic optimization was used in all the experiments presented later, although not the optimal it gives a sufficiently low CF for the explorative stage of this work.
The method consists of sampling the phases from a randomly generatoed distribution and comput the crest factor.
The process is repeated a certain number of time (e.g. 50 or 100) and the phase that produce the lowest crest factor kept.
A summary of the main method for crest-factor minimization present in the literature are described in the Appendix \ref{ch:crest factor}.

\subsection{AWG memory limitations and splitting strategy}
\label{sec:multisine split}
Benchtop Waveform generators consist of electronic circuits contructed around a digital-to-analog converter (DAC).
An integrated modern DAC chip has a buffer memory in which the waveform values are written and during the generation such memory is read in loop (called look-up tabel generation).  
Commonly, commercial devices have a buffer memory consisting of 12 to 32 kSa that is quite small for the excitations used in batteries that might span 6-7 decades (at least for the laboratory scale). 
A solution used in digital signal processing devices (used for example for the playback of audio files in any electronic device) is called direct digital synthesis (DDS) that creates a direct communication between a DAC and a micro-controller memory. 
This option require to be proficient in the programming and setting of micro-controller, specifically synchronizing the components to the same clock. 

In our laboratory we use instead a dedicated bench-top Arbitrary Waveform Generator (AWG) with two channels each with 64MSa; a huge upgrade to conventional integrated DACs. 
This amount of memory allow multi-sine waveforms containing at least 6 decades. 
To study batteries, though, my desire was to have seven or more decades fo frequency range ion the multi-sine and being able to switch between two waveforms optimized specifically for a chronopotentiometry and a chronoamperometry. 
The solution I came up with is to divide a multi-sine waveform into two waveforms and use the ability of the generator to sum the signals of the two channels ("dual channel mode") at output.  
The two separate waves have far less number of samples and the two types of multisines can easilty fit in the memory of the device.

As an example, let's consider a seven-decade multisine waveform from 10mHz to 100kHz, generated at 1MHz gives a waveform of 100MSa. 
Using 10 times the value of the maxium frequency is giving us good results; the literature advices even to use 100 times to avoid spectral leackage. 
Keysight devices have a proprietary 'TrueForm' technology to reduce jitter that might help with limiting the spectral leakage in the final signal.  
Generating one single braodband waveform correspond to 100 MSa (obtained as $period \times f_{gen}$). 
Dividing the frequency band at the value of 100Hz creates two smaller multisines: one from 10mHz to 100Hz (not included) generated at 1000Hz (size 100kSa), and a second one from exactly 100Hz to 100kHz generated a 1MHz that, chosing a period of 1s, leads to a waveform of 100kSa. 
The two smaller multisine takes up only 200kSa of the generator memory to produce the same excitation of the complete multiusine of 100MSa.
Figure \ref{fig:multisine-split-time-domain} reports the origianl and reduced multisines in the time-domain.

\begin{figure}[h]

    A single wideband multisine (right) is reproduced by summing two narrower-band multisines (left), each fitting within the memory constraints of the waveform generator.
    This strategy enables excitation spanning multiple decades of frequency without loss of spectral coherence.}

In the example I used two arbitrary parameters: the 100Hz boundary frequency and the 1s period of the high band waveform. 
For my case 100Hz sits in the middle so this might be adjusted case by case with the same rationale. 
Regarding the period of the second wave, one might think of using 0.01 second to have one exact period of the lowest frequency. 
One must now consider that the frequencies of the two waves should follow the same rules described before to avoid intermodulation and such, being made from a sequence of harmonics. 
Logarithmic sequences optimized for intermodulation tend to be sparser on the low frequency part and denser later. 
If for both waveform one generates the frequencies starting from the harmonic of 1, the measured impedance spectra would have a sparser points distribution in the decade from 100Hz and 1kHz where the high band multi-sine waveform starts. 

To get a uniform distibution of points, I generated the high frequency series starting from 1 Hz and discarded the frequencies until 10Hz (not included), since they are already present in the low band waveform. 
Then, the period of the waveform must be of 1Hz otherwise there will be spectral leakage in the final excitation (note that the frequencies are all integer multiples of 1Hz). 
The sum of the two shorter waveforms generated with this method have the exact distribution of a signle-gnerated wave. 
The effect of different strategies for restarting harmonic sequences when splitting a broadband multisine is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:splitting-design}.

\section{Automation software}
\label{sec:software}

\subsection{Problem statement}
Dynamic impedance spectroscopy measurements performed during battery aging experiments extend over time scales of days to weeks and generate continuous, high-bandwidth voltage and current data streams. When broadband excitation is applied throughout cycling, the resulting acquisition rate rapidly exceeds the available volatile memory of standard data acquisition systems. As a consequence, the feasibility of long-duration non-stationary impedance experiments is not limited by electrochemical stability, but by the ability to sustain uninterrupted signal acquisition and storage over arbitrarily long time horizons.

In conventional laboratory workflows, data acquisition is often constrained to fixed-length recordings whose duration is dictated by the available RAM of the acquisition device or host computer. This approach is incompatible with aging studies, where impedance must be tracked continuously across hundreds of electrochemical techniques and repeated charge-discharge cycles. Without an automated strategy for segmenting, storing, and reinitializing acquisition buffers, dynamic impedance measurements would be restricted to short snapshots, preventing systematic observation of slow degradation phenomena.

For this reason, automation in the present work is conceived primarily as a memory-management and scalability mechanism rather than as an operational convenience. The software framework developed here enables the acquisition of multi-frequency signals over arbitrarily long experiments by periodically transferring completed data blocks from volatile memory to persistent storage, while maintaining phase coherence and continuity of the excitation. This approach allows non-stationary impedance spectroscopy to be embedded seamlessly into long-term cycling protocols, making it possible to observe impedance evolution as a function of state of charge and state of health without imposing artificial limits on experiment duration.

Before I joined the ESECS group, the acquisition of multi-frequency cells signals was performed setting the three instruments manually and indipendelty, and streaming the signal from the acquisition device to the computer with a procedural MATLAB script calling the PicoTech SDK.
This approach binds the maximum signals length to the computer RAM because saving directly to disk is not possible for the slow speed of writing into mechanical hard drives compared to the high streaming frequency.
Although this works for short measurment like the characterization of material reported in Chapters \ref{chap:LTP} and \ref{chap:capacitor}, my final goal was the monitoring of battery aging upon many cycles, therfore I needed to develop an alternative strategy: at the end of each half-cycle (or electrochemical technique in a sequence) the signals from the RAM is stored to disk and the acquisition continue on the RAM.
This approach works until the signals of one half-cycle fit the memory, a constrain eliminated with the on-line computation that is the discussed in next Chapter \ref{On-line computation}.

The coordination of the electrochemical sequence, excitation waveform, and data acquisition is implemented through a central software control loop.
This loop continuously monitors the execution state of the potentiostat, synchronizes the activation of the multisine excitation, and manages the transfer of acquired data from volatile memory to persistent storage.
By reacting to technique completion events and loop counters, the control logic ensures uninterrupted acquisition while preserving phase coherence between excitation and response signals.

To make the automation possible, each instrument must be indipendently excecute its routine in a dedicated thtoead while its action being triggered from the main control loop.
The typical approach is to implement each instruments as a \emph{worker} object, which excecutes operations inside a \emph{while True} block that terminates with a pause during which the main thread is allowed to execute the code of another worker object. 
The multi-threading standard library of Python \verb|multithreading| is capable of automatically manage all the objects without user indication in the code (compare to the other library like \verb|asyncio|).

A worker object has a propriety with the name \verb|running| that can be used from other object to check their status. 
This idea is at the base of all the library used to syncronize the potentiostat, waveform generator and scope. 
Given the modularity of the apporach presented here, it is possible to use different hardware to perform the experiment in the same manner, providing a simlar interface is similar.

\section{Summary and Contribution}

This Chapter has presented the experimental framework developed for the acquisition of non-stationary electrochemical impedance data. The focus was deliberately placed on the hardware integration, excitation design, sampling strategy, and software automation required to enable dynamic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy under realistic battery operating conditions.

A central technical contribution of this work is the development of a strategy to generate long, arbitrary broadband excitation signals using commercial laboratory instrumentation. 
By exploiting the dual-channel capabilities and large onboard memory of modern arbitrary waveform generators, and by introducing a frequency-subdivision approach, it was possible to synthesize multisine excitations spanning up to seven decades of frequency while preserving spectral coherence and avoiding intermodulation artifacts. 
This capability exceeds what is typically achievable with conventional embedded DAC architectures and is essential for broadband impedance characterization of electrochemical systems.

Equally important is the automation layer developed to coordinate the potentiostat, waveform generator, and high-speed acquisition device over long-duration cycling experiments. 
A modular, open-source software ecosystem was created to manage instrument synchronization, multisine activation, continuous data streaming, buffering, and metadata logging without user intervention. 
This infrastructure enables uninterrupted acquisition of multi-frequency voltage and current signals over many charge--discharge cycles, a prerequisite for studying impedance evolution during battery aging.
The ability to reliably acquire long, synchronized, broadband datasets under controlled electrochemical conditions was a limiting factor in dynamic impedance spectroscopy up to this moment. 
This work establishes a practical and extensible platform for time-resolved impedance analysis in batteries and other electrochemical systems.
