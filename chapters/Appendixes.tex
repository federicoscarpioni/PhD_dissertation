\chapter{Impedance of electrical elements and basic circuits}
\label{app:imp elements}

\section{Basic circuit elements}

The impedance of ideal lumped elements in the frequency domain is defined as follows.

\paragraph{Resistor}
\begin{equation}
    Z_R = R,
\end{equation}
a purely real impedance, independent of frequency and without phase shift.

\paragraph{Capacitor}
\begin{equation}
    Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C},
\end{equation}
for which the current leads the voltage by $90^\circ$. Capacitors block direct current and exhibit decreasing impedance with increasing frequency.

\paragraph{Inductor}
\begin{equation}
    Z_L = j\omega L,
\end{equation}
where the voltage leads the current by $90^\circ$ and the impedance increases linearly with frequency.
\section{Series and parallel combinations}

For elements connected in series, impedances add directly:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\mathrm{series}} = \sum_k Z_k.
\end{equation}

For elements connected in parallel, admittances add:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{parallel}}} = \sum_k \frac{1}{Z_k}.
\end{equation}

A frequently used kernel for battery interfaces is the Randles circuit, consisting of a solution resistance $R_s$ in series with a parallel combination of charge-transfer resistance $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ and double-layer capacitance $C_{\mathrm{dl}}$, optionally augmented by a diffusion element.

\section{Equivalent-circuit signatures}

The most common impedance features encountered in electrochemical systems can be interpreted as follows.

\paragraph{Ohmic resistance}
The high-frequency intercept on the real axis corresponds to the uncompensated ohmic resistance $R_s$, which includes contributions from electrolyte, separator, and electrical connections.

\paragraph{Charge-transfer process}
A charge-transfer resistance $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ in parallel with a double-layer capacitance $C_{\mathrm{dl}}$ (or constant-phase element) produces a semicircle in the Nyquist plot, with a characteristic frequency
\[
\omega \approx \frac{1}{R_{\mathrm{ct}} C_{\mathrm{dl}}}.
\]

\paragraph{Diffusion}
Semi-infinite diffusion is represented by a Warburg impedance:
\begin{equation}
    Z_W = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{j\omega}},
\end{equation}
which appears as a $45^\circ$ line in the Nyquist plot.

For finite-length diffusion, the impedance bends toward a capacitive response at low frequency:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{W,\mathrm{FL}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{j\omega}} 
    \tanh\!\left(\sqrt{j\omega \tau_D}\right),
\end{equation}
or equivalently with a $\coth$ formulation, depending on the boundary conditions.

\section{Impedance representations}

The impedance magnitude and phase are defined as
\begin{equation}
    |Z(\omega)| = \sqrt{ (\Re Z)^2 + (\Im Z)^2 },
    \qquad
    \phi(\omega) = \arctan\!\left( \frac{\Im Z}{\Re Z} \right).
\end{equation}

In a Nyquist representation, $-\Im(Z)$ is plotted versus $\Re(Z)$, with frequency as an implicit parameter.
In a Bode representation, both magnitude and phase are plotted explicitly as functions of frequency, which is particularly useful for identifying overlapping processes through their characteristic time constants.


\chapter{Literature chronological overview}
\label{app:literature review stft}

With the rapid development of digital electronics and computational capabilities in the late 1980s and early 1990s, several research groups revisited the problem of measuring electrochemical impedance under non-stationary conditions.
A common experimental architecture progressively emerged, consisting of a potentiostat or galvanostat, an arbitrary waveform generator, and a digital data-acquisition system.
Within this framework, broadband excitation signals and time-frequency analysis enabled the estimation of instantaneous or time-resolved impedance during electrochemical drifts.

\section*{Early multisine implementations (1992--1997)}

The first systematic works on time-resolved impedance spectroscopy using broadband perturbations were reported by Popkirov and Schindler in the early 1990s.
In 1992, they introduced a custom-built potentiostat capable of operating in two distinct frequency ranges (100~kHz--100~Hz and 0.76~Hz--0.76~mHz) and demonstrated the feasibility of impedance measurements under non-stationary conditions using multisine excitation~\cite{popkirovNewImpedanceSpectrometer1992}.
Subsequent studies established that multisine signals composed of dozens of frequency components provide superior signal-to-noise ratio compared with alternative broadband waveforms~\cite{popkirovOptimizationPerturbationSignal1993a}.

A crucial contribution of this work was the validation of impedance estimation in the presence of nonlinearities and system non-stationarity~\cite{popkirovValidationExperimentalData1993a, popkirovEffectSampleNonlinearity1995a}.
These methodological developments were applied to a wide range of electrochemical systems, including electropolymerization~\cite{popkirovInSituTimeResolved1993}, redox processes in conducting polymers~\cite{popkirovInsituImpedanceMeasurements1995, popkirovElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy1995, popkirovInvestigationConductingPolymer1997, garcia-belmonteDeterminationElectronicConductivity2003a}, silver dissolution~\cite{popkirovElectrodePotentialRedistribution1995, baltrunasTimeEvolutionActive1997}, and the benzophenone/benzophenone anion radical redox couple in various solvents~\cite{walczykInvestigationRedoxCouple1995}.
Both potential-sweep and galvanostatic drifts were employed, with multisine excitation typically spanning frequencies from 1~Hz to 31~kHz.

\section*{High-density multisine and experimental optimization (2000--2005)}

Between 2000 and 2005, Roy, Garland, Pettit, and co-workers significantly extended the multisine approach by introducing high-density perturbations composed of up to 133 sinusoidal components.
Their excitation design employed only odd harmonics, random phase distributions, and equal-amplitude components~\cite{waltersWeakAdsorptionAnions2001a}.
A detailed discussion of experimental constraints, parameter selection, and intrinsic limitations of the technique was provided in subsequent methodological studies~\cite{garlandAnalysisExperimentalConstraints2004}.

This framework was applied to investigate a broad range of surface and interfacial processes on gold and other metals, including bismuth deposition~\cite{garlandKineticAnalysisElectrosorption2002a}, indium deposition with emphasis on co-adsorption phenomena~\cite{pettitElectrodepositionIndiumMolybdenum2002}, methanol electro-oxidation~\cite{assiongbonElectrooxidationMethanolGold2005}, redox kinetics influenced by co-adsorption~\cite{emeryTimeResolvedImpedance2005}, and the chemical reduction of nickel~\cite{shiElectrocatalysisHypophosphiteNickel2014}.
Additional studies focused on surface planarization and reaction pathways for thallium~\cite{assiongbonChemicalRolesPeroxidebased2004, gorantlaChemicalEffectsChemical2005, pettitRoleIodateIons2005}, copper~\cite{luRelativeRoles22004, pettitTimeResolvedStudy2004, gorantlaCitricAcidComplexing2005}, and silver~\cite{assiongbonElectrooxidationMethanolGold2005}.
Time-resolved impedance was also coupled with optical diagnostics to study dynamic surface processes.

\section*{Dynamic impedance via short-time Fourier analysis (2003--2020)}

Starting in 2003, Darowicki, S\l{}ępski, and collaborators developed a parallel line of research focused on estimating instantaneous impedance using short-time Fourier transform (STFT) techniques.
Early implementations employed exponential chirp excitation signals in the frequency range 1320--144~Hz~\cite{darowickiDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2003a, darowickiContinuousFrequencyMethodMeasurement2003}, later transitioning to multisine perturbations~\cite{darowickiInvestigationsPassiveLayer2003}.
The group proposed multiple experimental configurations for dynamic impedance estimation~\cite{darowickiInstantaneousElectrochemicalImpedance2004a}.

This methodology was applied extensively to corrosion science, including the study of passive layers and pitting corrosion on stainless steels~\cite{darowickiEvaluationPittingCorrosion2004a, darowickiTimeDependencePit2004, krakowiakImpedanceInvestigationPassive2005, krakowiakImpedanceMetastablePitting2005, orlikowskiInstantaneousImpedanceMonitoring2015, rylInstantaneousImpedanceMonitoring2016, orlikowskiDeterminationPittingCorrosion2017, naroznyApplicationElectrochemicalImpedance2018, darowickiNovelApplicationDynamic2020a, rylInstantaneousImpedanceAnalysis2017}, metal alloys~\cite{arutunowElectricalMechanicalCharacterization2015, zielinskiApplicationMultisineNanoscale2015, wysockaInvestigationElectrochemicalBehavior2016, gawelImpedanceMonitoringCorrosion2017}, and stress corrosion cracking~\cite{darowickiDetectionStressCorrosion2004}.
Additional applications include conductive polymers~\cite{darowickiImpedanceCharacterizationProcess2004, darowickiDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2004}, hydrogen storage alloys, nickel-metal hydride batteries, and coating adhesion studies~\cite{burczykLocalElectrochemicalImpedance2018}.

The frequency range in these studies typically spanned 3~Hz to 20~kHz, later extended down to approximately 30~mHz.
Dynamic impedance was also combined with atomic force microscopy~\cite{zielinskiMultifrequencyNanoscaleImpedance2019, rylStudySurfaceTermination2016} and quartz crystal microbalance techniques~\cite{parasinskaApplicationQuartzCrystal2024}.
Further applications include fuel cells~\cite{slepskiImpedanceMonitoringFuel2015, darowickiInfluenceDynamicLoad2019, darowickiImpedanceHydrogenOxidation2020a} and redox-flow batteries~\cite{krakowiakThreeModesElectrochemical2024}.

\section*{System identification and variance-aware impedance estimation (2004--2018)}

In parallel, Schoukens, Pintelon, and co-workers introduced a rigorous system-identification framework to electrochemical impedance spectroscopy.
Beginning in 2004, they applied frequency-domain identification theory to impedance measurements on corroding metals using multisine excitation with low-frequency components down to 10~mHz~\cite{vangheemElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2004a, vangheemElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2006a, blajievImprovementImpedanceMeasurement2006}.
A detailed comparison between multisine and stepped-sine excitation was provided to validate measurement accuracy~\cite{vangheemInstantaneousImpedanceMeasurements2004}.

In 2009, the advantages of using only odd random-phase harmonics were demonstrated using multisine signals with up to 220 components spanning 10~mHz to 2~kHz~\cite{vaningelgemAdvantagesOddRandom2009}.
Particular emphasis was placed on quantitative variance estimation and on separating errors arising from noise, nonlinearity, and non-stationarity.
Earlier foundational work on excitation signal design and stepped-sine versus broadband methods~\cite{schoukensBroadbandSteppedSine1999, schoukensSurveyExcitationSignals1988a} underpinned this approach.

Applications included corrosion studies of copper~\cite{vaningelgemApplicationMultisineImpedance2008}, aluminium and stainless steel~\cite{breugelmansOddRandomPhase2010a, alvarez-pampliegaCorrosionStudyAlrich2014, jiOddRandomPhase2018, jiElectrochemicalRamanAnalysis2020}, adsorption phenomena on metals and oxides~\cite{blajievPotentiodynamicEISInvestigation2008, geboesInvestigationAdsorptionMechanism2015, hauffmanMeasuringAdsorptionEthanol2012}, self-assembled monolayers~\cite{hauffmanDynamicSituStudy2013, fernandezmaciaStudyElectronTransfer2014}, polymeric coatings~\cite{jorcinInvestigationSelfhealingProperties2010, breugelmansOddRandomPhase2010, woutersMonitoringInitialContact2021, meeusenFEMModellingPredict2023, madelatSituMonitoringLateral2023}, ferri/ferrocyanide kinetics~\cite{fernandezmaciaORPEISStudyTime2015}, biosensors~\cite{pauwelsNewMultisinebasedImpedimetric2016}, and alloy protection~\cite{meeusenUseOddRandom2018}.

\section*{Dynamic impedance coupled with cyclic voltammetry}

Sacci and Harrington introduced a multisine excitation spanning 1~Hz to 1.3~kHz, with selectively removed harmonics to suppress intermodulation effects, superimposed on cyclic voltammetry~\cite{sacciDynamicImpedanceFormic2009}.
The multisine amplitude decreased by a factor of two every two decades, following the design principles introduced by Popkirov.
This approach was applied to electrocatalytic reactions~\cite{holmUnderstandingReactionMechanisms2018, holmDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2019, holmUnderstandingReactionMechanisms2019, sacciDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2014} and later extended to droplet interface bilayers~\cite{sacciDisentanglingMemristiveMemcapacitive2022}.

\section*{Analog separation of drift and perturbation}

Pajkossy and Mészáros proposed an alternative architecture in which the potentiostat was custom-built to perform analog separation of the low-frequency drift and high-frequency perturbation~\cite{pajkossyConnectionCVsImpedance2020}.
The excitation consisted of a sweeping voltage combined with a multisine signal using odd prime frequencies in the range 18.3~Hz to 17.8~kHz.
Extensive theoretical analysis was provided on the coupling between cyclic voltammetry and impedance for quasi-reversible redox systems~\cite{pajkossyDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2019, pajkossyVoltammetryCoupledImpedance2020, pajkossyDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2021}.

\section*{Early embedded implementations}

Earlier efforts toward compact implementations include the work of Ivaska and co-workers, who adapted Smith’s technique into a smaller hardware platform~\cite{engblomExperiencesOnLineFourier1990}.
Hazi, Bond, and colleagues later extended this concept using microcomputer-based instrumentation capable of admittance and impedance measurements in the range 50~Hz to 50~kHz during staircase voltammetry~\cite{haziMicrocomputerbasedInstrumentationMultifrequency1997, schieweUnifiedApproachTrace1998}.

\chapter{Multisine crest factor minimization}
\label{ch:crest factor}
Multiple approaches have been proposed in the field to minimize the crest factor and each is a trade-off between speed and accuracy (find the absolute minimum) \cite{zappenApplicationTimeResolvedMultiSine2018}. 
The right method strongly depends on the type of problem and on the following characteristics: distribution of frequency (logarithmic or linear), number of points per decades, number of frequencies in the waveform and relative amplitude (flat, linear, logarithmic, arbitrary). 

The first to deal with the problem was Schroeder in 1990 whom proposed the analytical formula that carries his name:

$$
\varphi_m = \varphi _0 - 2 \pi \sum _{n=0} ^{m-1} (m-n) \frac{|a_m|^2}{\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}|a_k|^2}. 
$$

Since it is fast to calculate, it is often used as both  reference value and initial parameters for a following iterative optimization. Newman and Ojarand took the work forward with analytical formulas but without significant advantages \cite{ojarandRecentAdvancesCrest2017}.

As an alternative, randomization of the phases offers an equal or better crest factor but the computational time increases significantly with the number of frequencies.

Iterative algorithms achieve much better results. They belong to two families. One is based on the "clipping" method of the DFT peaks of the multi sine until the crest factor reaches a constant value \cite{yangImprovedCrestFactor2015}. 
The other consists in a mathematical optimization using metaheuristic approaches that perform better than derivative methods for such big parameters space (ex simulated annealing and genetic algorithm) \cite{janeiroPeakFactorOptimization2020}.

Recently Kallel et al. proposed a mixed method, though it gives better results than the analytical formula for small multisine of 3-4 decades \cite{batteries8100176}.

\chapter{Silicon carbonitride as energy storage material}
\label{app:SiCNO_background}

Silicon carbonitride (SiCN(O)) belongs to the class of polymer-derived ceramics obtained through the pyrolysis of preceramic polymers, typically polysilazanes or polysiloxanes, under inert atmosphere at temperatures in the range of 800-1200\,$^\circ$C. 
During pyrolysis, the organic precursor decomposes into an amorphous ceramic network composed of Si-N, Si-C, and, to a lesser extent, Si-O bonding environments, while simultaneously forming a carbon-rich phase with mixed sp$^2$/sp$^3$ character. 
The relative proportion of ceramic matrix and carbon domains, as well as the pore-size distribution, strongly depends on the precursor chemistry and on the thermal protocol used for crosslinking and pyrolysis. 
This synthetic route provides a higher level of structural control compared with hard carbons, which derive from the pyrolysis of organic precursors but lack the tunability offered by polymer-derived ceramics.

The resulting microstructure consists of a dual-phase material in which a turbostratic carbon phase percolates through a mechanically rigid amorphous ceramic backbone.
The carbon domains facilitate electronic conduction and provide high-surface-area adsorption sites for alkali ions, whereas the ceramic network ensures chemical and mechanical stability. 
The incorporation of nitrogen into the ceramic framework is particularly relevant for sodium storage, because Si-N bonding increases the affinity of the material for sodium atoms and reduces their binding energy relative to pure carbon surfaces. 
This, together with the presence of micropores and defective carbon environments, makes SiCN(O) a promising candidate for sodium-ion battery anodes.

The storage of sodium in SiCN(O) is generally attributed to a combination of three partially overlapping mechanisms. 
At relatively high potentials, adsorption of Na\textsuperscript{+} ions on defective carbon sites dominates, leading to a sloping voltage profile analogous to that of hard carbon. 
At intermediate potentials, ion insertion into micropores contributes significantly to the capacity, reflecting the accessible pore network created during pyrolysis. 
At lower potentials, and especially when cycling below 0\,V vs.\ Na/Na\textsuperscript{+}, several studies report an additional reversible capacity that has been interpreted as the partial deposition of metallic sodium inside confined pores. 
This hypothesis is supported by solid-state NMR, post-mortem microscopy, and cycling experiments showing that the additional low-potential capacity disappears when the microporosity is reduced during synthesis. 
Nonetheless, the precise nature of the low-potential storage mode remains debated, and may include quasi-metallic clusters, subnanometric Na aggregates, or deep insertion into defective carbon environments. 
Because the material is fully amorphous, these mechanisms are expected to coexist and evolve dynamically.

Characterizing SiCN(O) is challenging because the relevant electrochemical processes span a broad range of time scales and are strongly coupled to the material's heterogeneous microstructure.
Adsorption, pore filling, SEI formation, and possible metal deposition all occur in partially overlapping voltage windows, and the distribution of pore sizes affects both ion transport and interfacial kinetics. 
The SEI itself forms on chemically distinct surfaces (carbonaceous domains and ceramic phases), leading to spatially non-uniform interphase compositions and potentially time-dependent changes during cycling. 
As a result, traditional techniques such as galvanostatic cycling or ex situ surface analysis often provide incomplete or ambiguous information.

In this context, dynamic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (DEIS) is particularly well suited for studying SiCN(O). 
Because DEIS resolves the instantaneous impedance along a non-stationary potential trajectory, it can reveal transient kinetic events that are difficult to detect with steady-state techniques.
Sudden changes in characteristic time constants, transitions between adsorption- and insertion-dominated regimes, or the onset of fast transport pathways associated with quasi-metallic phases become visible as local impedance features that coincide with specific points of the voltage profile. 
Moreover, DEIS can track the evolution of SEI resistance during the first cycles, distinguish formation-related impedance from intrinsic electrode impedance, and allow a direct comparison between different synthesis batches or porosity levels without relying exclusively on capacity values. 
In the context of this thesis, DEIS was used to investigate whether such characteristic signatures could be associated with the hypothesized sodium storage mechanisms in SiCN(O), and in particular whether reversible sodium deposition within confined pores manifests as a measurable, transient reduction of the impedance.

The purpose of this appendix is not to provide a complete review of SiCN(O) electrochemistry, but rather to supply the structural and mechanistic background necessary to interpret the DEIS measurements presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:anodes}. 
Readers interested in a comprehensive treatment of the synthesis, structure, and electrochemical behavior of polymer-derived ceramics are referred to the foundational literature (Reinold et al.\ 2013; Sic et al.\ 2022; Cheng et al.\ 2021; Chen et al.\ 2022).

\chapter{Fabrication and Characterization of Microscopic Reference Electrodes}
\label{app:microRE}

\section{Design considerations}

The goal of the microscopic reference electrode (micro-RE) is to provide a stable potential probe inside a compact cell without perturbing the electric field or contributing significant impedance artefacts.  
This requires minimizing the exposed electrode surface, ensuring robust insulation along the wire length, and achieving uniform metal electrodeposition on the active tip.  
Because the reference electrode must be inserted within the separator stack of a pouch cell, flexibility and resistance to mechanical deformation are also essential.

For lithium systems, the most straightforward approach is to electrodeposit Li onto an insulated copper wire.  
For sodium systems, Na can be electrodeposited on Cu, although Na does not alloy with noble metals such as Au or Al; this limits the use of stable alloy-type references commonly used in lithium-ion research.

\section{Fabrication of micro-reference electrodes}

Three types of current collectors were tested:
\begin{itemize}
    \item manually insulated copper wire (diameter $\sim$130\,$\mu$m);
    \item polymer-coated 50\,$\mu$m Cu wire (Goodfellow);
    \item polymer-coated 50\,$\mu$m Au wire (Goodfellow).
\end{itemize}

The manually insulated wires were prepared by sandwiching the copper wire between two thermoplastic sealing strips typically used for pouch-cell fabrication.  
The polymer was heat-fused using an ultrasonic sealer.  
Excess polymer was removed with a scalpel to expose a circular cross-section at the tip.  
Achieving a symmetric circular opening required practice; non-perpendicular cuts resulted in elliptical exposed areas and increased electrode surface.

Commercial 50\,$\mu$m wires provided reproducible insulation thickness but posed mechanical challenges: they were too thin for crocodile clips, prone to breaking during sealing, and difficult to integrate into current collectors.  
Attempts to solder these wires to metallic tabs using ultrasonic welding were unsuccessful, as excessive pressure broke the wire, while insufficient pressure resulted in incomplete bonding.

An alternative approach, sealing both the current collector tab and the wire tip with thermoplastic strips, ensured electrical contact and mechanical stability but compromised the integrity of the exposed area, leading to partial copper exposure during use.

\section{Electrodeposition procedures}

For Li micro-REs, electrodeposition was performed in a two-electrode configuration using a 12\,mm Li metal disk as counter electrode.  
A two-step protocol was adopted: 10\,min of potentiostatic deposition at \mbox{$-100$\,mV}, followed by 3\,h of galvanostatic deposition at \mbox{$-50$\,\textmu A}.  
Afterward, the electrode was left at open-circuit potential.  
Electrodes fabricated by this method remained stable for several months, as verified by OCV tests.

Sodium deposition followed analogous protocols.  
However, Na deposition in organic electrolytes competes with electrolyte reduction, and the formation of a heterogeneous SEI layer frequently destabilized the deposited Na.  
In several cases, the Na film dissolved partially over time, causing the reference potential to drift back toward that of bare copper.

For Au-wire substrates, alloy formation with Na was not achieved, despite attempts to reproduce literature procedures involving extremely low cathodic currents (tens of picoamperes).  
This difficulty is attributed to the presence of exposed regions on the metallic current collector tab.

\section{Electrochemical stability and failure modes}

Several failure modes were observed during extended cycling:

\begin{itemize}
    \item drift of the reference potential due to partial dissolution of deposited metal;
    \item micro-shorting or leakage currents caused by incomplete insulation;
    \item mechanical deformation of the wire during pouch-cell sealing, especially for 50\,$\mu$m substrates;
    \item artefacts in impedance spectra caused by protrusion of the wire tip into the separator or deformation of the electrode stack;
    \item sensitivity of the Na micro-RE to electrolyte purity and water contamination.
\end{itemize}

These issues limit reproducibility and currently prevent quantitative comparison across cells.  
Nonetheless, in many cases the micro-REs remained sufficiently stable over the duration of a DEIS measurement to enable meaningful qualitative analysis.

\section{Implications for future work}

The results indicate that while micro-reference electrodes are feasible for operando DEIS in pouch cells, achieving reproducibility requires improvements in wire insulation, mechanical protection, and electrodeposition quality.  
Potential strategies include the use of metallic meshes as mechanically stable supports, the adoption of laser-cut insulating sleeves, or the exploration of alloy-type reference systems when compatible with the chemistry under study.
