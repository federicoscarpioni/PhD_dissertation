\chapter{Impedance of electrical elements and basic circuits}
\label{app:imp elements}

\subsection{Basic circuit elements}
Resistor:
\begin{equation}
    Z_R = R \quad \text{(real; no phase shift).}
    \label{eq:ZR}
\end{equation}

Capacitor:
\begin{equation}
    Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C}, \quad \text{current leads voltage by $90^\circ$; blocks DC, passes AC with decreasing impedance as $\omega$ increases.}
    \label{eq:ZC}
\end{equation}

Inductor:
\begin{equation}
    Z_L = j\omega L, \quad \text{voltage leads current by $90^\circ$.}
    \label{eq:ZL}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Series/parallel combinations}

For elements in series, impedances add:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{\mathrm{series}} = \sum_k Z_k.
    \label{eq:Zseries}
\end{equation}
For elements in parallel, admittances add:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{Z_{\mathrm{parallel}}} = \sum_k \frac{1}{Z_k}.
    \label{eq:Zparallel}
\end{equation}
A useful kernel for battery interfaces is the Randles circuit: $R_s$ in series with $(R_{\mathrm{ct}} \parallel C_{\mathrm{dl}})$ and, optionally, a Warburg element for diffusion.

\subsection{Equivalent-circuit signatures}

Ohmic resistance ($R_s$): high-frequency intercept (electrolyte, separator, leads).

Charge transfer ($R_{\mathrm{ct}}$) in parallel with double layer ($C_{\mathrm{dl}}$ or CPE): one (or more) semicircles in Nyquist with apex near $\omega \approx 1/(R_{\mathrm{ct}} C_{\mathrm{dl}})$.

Diffusion (Warburg):
\begin{equation}
    Z_W = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{j\omega}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \text{45$^\circ$ line in Nyquist.}
    \label{eq:warburg}
\end{equation}
Finite-length diffusion bends toward vertical (capacitive) at low frequency:
\begin{equation}
    Z_{W,\mathrm{FL}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{j\omega}} \tanh\!\left(\sqrt{j\omega \tau_D}\right) \quad \text{or} \quad \coth\!\left(\sqrt{j\omega \tau_D}\right),
    \label{eq:warburg_fl}
\end{equation}
depending on boundary condition.


\subsection{Impedance plots}

Magnitude and phase are
\begin{equation}
    |Z(\omega)| = \sqrt{ \big(\Re Z\big)^2 + \big(\Im Z\big)^2 },
    \qquad
    \phi(\omega) = \arctan\!\left( \frac{\Im Z}{\Re Z} \right).
    \label{eq:mag_phase}
\end{equation}
Nyquist: plot $-\Im(Z)$ vs $\Re(Z)$; each point corresponds to a frequency. Bode: plot $|Z(\omega)|$ and $\phi(\omega)$ vs $\omega$; useful for separating overlapping processes by corner frequencies.

\chapter{Literature chronological overview}
\label{app:literature review stft}
In the 90s and early two thousand, with the advancement in computing power and electronic instrumentation, multiple research groups regain interest in the topic.
They commonly share the instrumental setup made of three independent devices connected together: a potentiostat/galvanostat, an arbitrary waveform generator and a digital signal acquisition unit.

The first works were from G.S. Popkirov and R.N Schindler \cite{popkirovNewImpedanceSpectrometer1992} in 1992 that used an home made potentiostat capable of two frequency ranges: 100kHz-100Hz or 0.76Hz-0.76mHz.
They also show that a multisine wave (of 42 components) gives the best signal to noise ratio compared other waveforms \cite{popkirovOptimizationPerturbationSignal1993a} and the validation of the measurement for the interference of non-linearities \cite{popkirovValidationExperimentalData1993a} \cite{popkirovEffectSampleNonlinearity1995a}.
The authors applied the method for electropolymerization \cite{popkirovInSituTimeResolved1993}, the characterization of redox process in conductive polymers \cite{popkirovInsituImpedanceMeasurements1995} \cite{popkirovElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy1995} \cite{popkirovInvestigationConductingPolymer1997} \cite{garcia-belmonteDeterminationElectronicConductivity2003a}, silver dissolution \cite{popkirovElectrodePotentialRedistribution1995} \cite{baltrunasTimeEvolutionActive1997} and Benzophenone/Benzophenone anion radical couple in different solutions \cite{walczykInvestigationRedoxCouple1995}.
They used both sweeping voltage and galavnostatic techniques as main drift and a multisine between 1 Hz and 31 kHz.

From 2000 to 2005 D. Roy, J. E. Garland, C.M. Pettit and collegues proposed measuremnet setup using a 133 components multi-sine perturbation  of only odd frequencies, random phase and equal amplitude \cite{waltersWeakAdsorptionAnions2001a}. 
They also discussess in details how to choose experimental parameters and the limitation of such technique \cite{garlandAnalysisExperimentalConstraints2004}. 
They applied the method to study difference processes on gold such as deposition of bismuth \cite{garlandKineticAnalysisElectrosorption2002a} and indium with focus on co-adsorbtion, electro-oxidation of methanol \cite{assiongbonElectrooxidationMethanolGold2005}, redox couple kinetics with focus on co-adsorbtion \cite{emeryTimeResolvedImpedance2005} and chemical reduction of Nickel \cite{shiElectrocatalysisHypophosphiteNickel2014}. 
They also coupled time-resolved impedance with optical measurements during indium deposition on molybdenum \cite{pettitElectrodepositionIndiumMolybdenum2002}. 
Furthermore, they studied surface reaction and effect planarization for different metals: Tallium \cite{assiongbonChemicalRolesPeroxidebased2004} \cite{gorantlaChemicalEffectsChemical2005} \cite{pettitRoleIodateIons2005}, Copper \cite{luRelativeRoles22004} \cite{pettitTimeResolvedStudy2004} \cite{gorantlaCitricAcidComplexing2005} and Silver \cite{assiongbonElectrooxidationMethanolGold2005} .

From 2003, K. Darowicki, P. Slepski and their colleagues, develop different experimental setups for the estimation of instantanous impedance \cite{darowickiInvestigationsPassiveLayer2003} \cite{darowickiInstantaneousElectrochemicalImpedance2004a}. 
The impedance is still estimated via short-time Fourier transform, at first emplying exponential chirp excitation (in the range 1320-144 Hz) \cite{darowickiDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2003a} \cite{darowickiContinuousFrequencyMethodMeasurement2003} \cite{darowickiDeterminationElectrodeImpedance2004a} and later with multisine \cite{darowickiInvestigationsPassiveLayer2003}. 
The prolific work conducted by the group touches multiple applications: corrosion evaluation on stainless steel \cite{darowickiEvaluationPittingCorrosion2004a} \cite{darowickiTimeDependencePit2004} \cite{krakowiakImpedanceInvestigationPassive2005} \cite{krakowiakImpedanceMetastablePitting2005} \cite{orlikowskiInstantaneousImpedanceMonitoring2015} \cite{rylInstantaneousImpedanceMonitoring2016} \cite{orlikowskiDeterminationPittingCorrosion2017} \cite{naroznyApplicationElectrochemicalImpedance2018} \cite{darowickiNovelApplicationDynamic2020a} \cite{rylInstantaneousImpedanceAnalysis2017} and metal alloys \cite{arutunowElectricalMechanicalCharacterization2015} \cite{zielinskiApplicationMultisineNanoscale2015} \cite{wysockaInvestigationElectrochemicalBehaviour2016} \cite{gawelImpedanceMonitoringCorrosion2017}, monitoring of the crack of corrosion layers on stainless steel \cite{darowickiDetectionStressCorrosion2004}, characterization of conductive polymers \cite{darowickiImpedanceCharacterizationProcess2004} \cite{darowickiDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2004}, investigating the hydrogen storage alloy [2010], monitoring the discharge of nickel/nickel hydride batteries [2013],  monitoring the adhesion of organic coating to stainless steel and galvanic coupling \cite{burczykLocalElectrochemicalImpedance2018}.
The frequency range of the multi-sine of these works is about 3 Hz - 20 kHz. 
The method was also coupled with atomic force microscopy for the study of surface terminations of boron-doped diamond \cite{zielinskiMultifrequencyNanoscaleImpedance2019} \cite{rylStudySurfaceTermination2016} and the quartz crystal microbalance \cite{parasinskaApplicationQuartzCrystal2024}. 
The researchers applied the techniques to study fuel cell under different operation \cite{slepskiImpedanceMonitoringFuel2015} \cite{darowickiInfluenceDynamicLoad2019} \cite{darowickiImpedanceHydrogenOxidation2020a} and redox-flow batteries \cite{krakowiakThreeModesElectrochemical2024}. 
Here the frequency range was extended to 4.5 kHz-0.3 Hz (or 30 mHz). 

In 2004 J. Schouckens and R. Pintelon started to apply their theoretical framework [???] of system identification in the frequency domain characterization of electrochemical systems with A. Hubin to obtain the electrochemical impedance of corroding metals using multisine perurbations with low frequency components down to 0.01Hz \cite{vangheemElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2004a}\cite{vangheemElectrochemicalImpedanceSpectroscopy2006a} \cite{blajievImprovementImpedanceMeasurement2006}. 
It is interesting how they compare multi-sine and stepped sine consecutive measurement to validate the experiment in \cite{vangheemInstantaneousImpedanceMeasurements2004}. 
In 2009 they discussed the advantage of using only the odd-number harmonics in the sequence of summed sinusoidal with 220 components between 10mHz and 2kHz \cite{vaningelgemAdvantagesOddRandom2009}. 
In particular, they put attention on the quantitative estimation of the impedance variance and the error due to the non-linearities and non-stationarity of the system.
They also discussed the difference in estimating the frequency response function with multi-frequency waveform and stepped sine though simulation adressing signal to noise ratio and measurement time \cite{schoukensBroadbandSteppedSine1999} and  compared different excitation signals including periodic, transient and aperiodic signals \cite{schoukensSurveyExcitationSignals1988a}.
Their method was applied for the study of multiple systems: corrosion of Copper\cite{vaningelgemApplicationMultisineImpedance2008}, Alluminum and stainless steel \cite{breugelmansOddRandomPhase2010a} \cite{alvarez-pampliegaCorrosionStudyAlrich2014} \cite{jiOddRandomPhase2018} \cite{jiElectrochemicalRamanAnalysis2020}, the adsorbtion of molecules on metals \cite{blajievPotentiodynamicEISInvestigation2008} \cite{geboesInvestigationAdsorptionMechanism2015} and oxides \cite{hauffmanMeasuringAdsorptionEthanol2012}, self-assembling monolayers \cite{hauffmanDynamicSituStudy2013}\cite{fernandezmaciaStudyElectronTransfer2014}, polymeric coatings \cite{jorcinInvestigationSelfhealingProperties2010}\cite{breugelmansOddRandomPhase2010}\cite{woutersMonitoringInitialContact2021} \cite{meeusenFEMModellingPredict2023}\cite{madelatSituMonitoringLateral2023}, ferri/ferrocyanide reaction on gold \cite{fernandezmaciaORPEISStudyTime2015}, aptomer as biosensors \cite{pauwelsNewMultisinebasedImpedimetric2016} and alloy protection \cite{meeusenUseOddRandom2018}. 

In 2009 R.L Sacci and D. A. Harrington proposed a multi-sine with 1Hz - 1.3 kHz with removed harmonics to avoid intermodulation following Popkirov 1993  and amplitude decreasing by a factor of two every two decades, superimposed to cylic voltammetry.
They used the method to study electroxidation of metanol  \cite{sacciDynamicImpedanceFormic2009}
\cite{holmUnderstandingReactionMechanisms2018} \cite{holmDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2019} \cite{holmUnderstandingReactionMechanisms2019}, electrocatalytic reactions \cite{sacciDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2014} and droplet interface bilayers \cite{sacciDisentanglingMemristiveMemcapacitive2022}.

In 2020 T. Pajkossy and G. Mészáros proposed a set-up in which the potentiostat is custom build in order to analogically seperate the drift and oscillations.
The input controll is still made by the superposition of a main drift, sweeping voltage, and small multi-frequency excitation with odd prime frequencies in the band 18.3 Hz - 17.8 kHz \cite{pajkossyConnectionCVsImpedance2020}. 
The four outputs are recorded with analog to digital converter. 
The researchers did also an extensive theoretical evaluation of the coupling of cyclic voltammetry and impedance for the estimation of charge transfer resistance for quasi-reversible redox systems \cite{pajkossyDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2019}  \cite{pajkossyVoltammetryCoupledImpedance2020} \cite{pajkossyDynamicElectrochemicalImpedance2021}. 

In 1990, Ivaska and collegues ported the technique of Smith into a smaller form factor with details on the hardware \cite{engblomExperiencesOnLineFourier1990}.
In 1996 Hazi, Bond and collegues, extended the system to modern hardare for the measurement of admittance or impedance in the frequency range 50Hz-50kHz during staircase voltammetry \cite{haziMicrocomputerbasedInstrumentationMultifrequency1997} \cite{schieweUnifiedApproachTrace1998}.

\chapter{Multisine crest factor minimization}
\label{ch:crest factor}
Multiple approaches has been proposed in the field to minimize the crest factor and each is a trade-off between speed and accuracy (find the absolute minimum). 
The right method strongly depends on the type of problem and on the following characteristics: distribution of frequency (logarithmic or linear), number of points per decades, number of frequencies in the waveform and relative amplitude (flat, linear, logarithmic, arbitrary). 

The first to deal with the problem was Schroeder in 1990 whom proposed the analytical formula that carries his name:

$$
\varphi_m = \varphi _0 - 2 \pi \sum _{n=0} ^{m-1} (m-n) \frac{|a_m|^2}{\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}|a_k|^2}. 
$$

Since it is fast to calculate, it is often used as both  reference value and initial parameters for a following iterative optimization. Newman and Ojarand took the work forward with analytical formulas but without significant advantages.

As an alternative, randomization of the phases offers an equal or better crest factor but the computational time increases significantly with the number of frequencies.

Iterative algorithms achieve much better results. They belong to two families. One is based on the "clipping" method of the DFT peaks of the multi sine until the crest factor reaches a constant value. 
The other consist in a mathematical optimization using metaheuristic approaches that perform better than derivative methods for such big parameters space (ex simulated annealing and genetic algorithm).

Recently Kallel et al. proposed a mixed method, though it gives better results than the analytical formula for small multisine of 3-4 decades.

\chapter{Background on Silicon Carbonitride as Negative Electrode Material}
\label{app:SiCNO_background}

Silicon carbonitride (SiCN(O)) belongs to the class of polymer-derived ceramics obtained through the pyrolysis of preceramic polymers, typically polysilazanes or polysiloxanes, under inert atmosphere at temperatures in the range of 800-1200\,$^\circ$C. 
During pyrolysis, the organic precursor decomposes into an amorphous ceramic network composed of Si-N, Si-C, and, to a lesser extent, Si-O bonding environments, while simultaneously forming a carbon-rich phase with mixed sp$^2$/sp$^3$ character. 
The relative proportion of ceramic matrix and carbon domains, as well as the pore-size distribution, strongly depends on the precursor chemistry and on the thermal protocol used for crosslinking and pyrolysis. 
This synthetic route provides a higher level of structural control compared with hard carbons, which derive from the pyrolysis of organic precursors but lack the tunability offered by polymer-derived ceramics.

The resulting microstructure consists of a dual-phase material in which a turbostratic carbon phase percolates through a mechanically rigid amorphous ceramic backbone.
The carbon domains facilitate electronic conduction and provide high-surface-area adsorption sites for alkali ions, whereas the ceramic network ensures chemical and mechanical stability. 
The incorporation of nitrogen into the ceramic framework is particularly relevant for sodium storage, because Si-N bonding increases the affinity of the material for sodium atoms and reduces their binding energy relative to pure carbon surfaces. 
This, together with the presence of micropores and defective carbon environments, makes SiCN(O) a promising candidate for sodium-ion battery anodes.

The storage of sodium in SiCN(O) is generally attributed to a combination of three partially overlapping mechanisms. 
At relatively high potentials, adsorption of Na\textsuperscript{+} ions on defective carbon sites dominates, leading to a sloping voltage profile analogous to that of hard carbon. 
At intermediate potentials, ion insertion into micropores contributes significantly to the capacity, reflecting the accessible pore network created during pyrolysis. 
At lower potentials, and especially when cycling below 0\,V vs.\ Na/Na\textsuperscript{+}, several studies report an additional reversible capacity that has been interpreted as the partial deposition of metallic sodium inside confined pores. 
This hypothesis is supported by solid-state NMR, post-mortem microscopy, and cycling experiments showing that the additional low-potential capacity disappears when the microporosity is reduced during synthesis. 
Nonetheless, the precise nature of the low-potential storage mode remains debated, and may include quasi-metallic clusters, subnanometric Na aggregates, or deep insertion into defective carbon environments. 
Because the material is fully amorphous, these mechanisms are expected to coexist and evolve dynamically.

Characterizing SiCN(O) is challenging because the relevant electrochemical processes span a broad range of time scales and are strongly coupled to the material's heterogeneous microstructure.
Adsorption, pore filling, SEI formation, and possible metal deposition all occur in partially overlapping voltage windows, and the distribution of pore sizes affects both ion transport and interfacial kinetics. 
The SEI itself forms on chemically distinct surfaces (carbonaceous domains and ceramic phases), leading to spatially non-uniform interphase compositions and potentially time-dependent changes during cycling. 
As a result, traditional techniques such as galvanostatic cycling or ex situ surface analysis often provide incomplete or ambiguous information.

In this context, Dynamic Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (DEIS) is particularly well suited for studying SiCN(O). 
Because DEIS resolves the instantaneous impedance along a non-stationary potential trajectory, it can reveal transient kinetic events that are difficult to detect with steady-state techniques.
Sudden changes in characteristic time constants, transitions between adsorption- and insertion-dominated regimes, or the onset of fast transport pathways associated with quasi-metallic phases become visible as local impedance features that coincide with specific points of the voltage profile. 
Moreover, DEIS can track the evolution of SEI resistance during the first cycles, distinguish formation-related impedance from intrinsic electrode impedance, and allow a direct comparison between different synthesis batches or porosity levels without relying exclusively on capacity values. 
In the context of this thesis, DEIS was used to investigate whether such characteristic signatures could be associated with the hypothesized sodium storage mechanisms in SiCN(O), and in particular whether reversible sodium deposition within confined pores manifests as a measurable, transient reduction of the impedance.

The purpose of this appendix is not to provide a complete review of SiCN(O) electrochemistry, but rather to supply the structural and mechanistic background necessary to interpret the DEIS measurements presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:anodes}. 
Readers interested in a comprehensive treatment of the synthesis, structure, and electrochemical behaviour of polymer-derived ceramics are referred to the foundational literature (Reinold et al.\ 2013; Sic et al.\ 2022; Cheng et al.\ 2021; Chen et al.\ 2022).

\chapter{Fabrication and Characterization of Microscopic Reference Electrodes}
\label{app:microRE}

\section{Design considerations}

The goal of the microscopic reference electrode (micro-RE) is to provide a stable potential probe inside a compact cell without perturbing the electric field or contributing significant impedance artefacts.  
This requires minimizing the exposed electrode surface, ensuring robust insulation along the wire length, and achieving uniform metal electrodeposition on the active tip.  
Because the reference electrode must be inserted within the separator stack of a pouch cell, flexibility and resistance to mechanical deformation are also essential.

For lithium systems, the most straightforward approach is to electrodeposit Li onto an insulated copper wire.  
For sodium systems, Na can be electrodeposited on Cu, although Na does not alloy with noble metals such as Au or Al; this limits the use of stable alloy-type references commonly used in lithium-ion research.

\section{Insulated wire fabrication}

Three types of wire substrates were tested:
\begin{itemize}
    \item manually insulated copper wire (diameter $\sim$130\,$\mu$m);
    \item polymer-coated 50\,$\mu$m Cu wire (Goodfellow);
    \item polymer-coated 50\,$\mu$m Au wire (Goodfellow).
\end{itemize}

The manually insulated wires were prepared by sandwiching the copper wire between two thermoplastic sealing strips typically used for pouch-cell fabrication.  
The polymer was heat-fused using an ultrasonic sealer.  
Excess polymer was removed with a scalpel to expose a circular cross-section at the tip.  
Achieving a symmetric circular opening required practice; non-perpendicular cuts resulted in elliptical exposed areas and increased electrode surface.

Commercial 50\,$\mu$m wires provided reproducible insulation thickness but posed mechanical challenges: they were too thin for crocodile clips, prone to breaking during sealing, and difficult to integrate into current collectors.  
Attempts to solder these wires to metallic tabs using ultrasonic welding were unsuccessful, as excessive pressure broke the wire, while insufficient pressure resulted in incomplete bonding.

An alternative approach, sealing both the current collector tab and the wire tip with thermoplastic strips, ensured electrical contact and mechanical stability but compromised the integrity of the exposed area, leading to partial copper exposure during use.

\section{Electrodeposition procedures}

For Li micro-REs, electrodeposition was performed in a two-electrode configuration using a 12\,mm Li metal disk as counter electrode.  
A two-step protocol was adopted: 10\,min of potentiostatic deposition at \mbox{$-100$\,mV}, followed by 3\,h of galvanostatic deposition at \mbox{$-50$\,\textmu A}.  
Afterward, the electrode was left at open-circuit potential.  
Electrodes fabricated by this method remained stable for several months, as verified by OCV tests.

Sodium deposition followed analogous protocols.  
However, Na deposition in organic electrolytes competes with electrolyte reduction, and the formation of a heterogeneous SEI layer frequently destabilized the deposited Na.  
In several cases, the Na film dissolved partially over time, causing the reference potential to drift back toward that of bare copper.

For Au-wire substrates, alloy formation with Na was not achieved, despite attempts to reproduce literature procedures involving extremely low cathodic currents (tens of picoamperes).  
This difficulty is attributed to the presence of exposed regions on the metallic current collector tab.

\section{Electrochemical stability and failure modes}

Several failure modes were observed during extended cycling:

\begin{itemize}
    \item drift of the reference potential due to partial dissolution of deposited metal;
    \item micro-shorting or leakage currents caused by incomplete insulation;
    \item mechanical deformation of the wire during pouch-cell sealing, especially for 50\,$\mu$m substrates;
    \item artefacts in impedance spectra caused by protrusion of the wire tip into the separator or deformation of the electrode stack;
    \item sensitivity of the Na micro-RE to electrolyte purity and water contamination.
\end{itemize}

These issues limit reproducibility and currently prevent quantitative comparison across cells.  
Nonetheless, in many cases the micro-REs remained sufficiently stable over the duration of a DEIS measurement to enable meaningful qualitative analysis.

\section{Implications for future work}

The results indicate that while micro-reference electrodes are feasible for operando DEIS in pouch cells, achieving reproducibility requires improvements in wire insulation, mechanical protection, and electrodeposition quality.  
Potential strategies include the use of metallic meshes as mechanically stable supports, the adoption of laser-cut insulating sleeves, or the exploration of alloy-type reference systems when compatible with the chemistry under study.
